## What the Subtitle Must Do

A KDP subtitle has two jobs that can conflict: it must serve **Amazon's search algorithm** (keyword density) and it must serve **the reader skimming the cover** (clarity + intrigue). For this book, the target reader — 40–65, Christian or spiritually curious, reads quantum physics and religious history nonfiction — is more likely to be browsing categories than running keyword searches. That slightly shifts the balance: the subtitle needs to earn a click from a browser, not just a keyword match.

The book's core tension is a scientist who built an instrument to *extract* data and must learn instead to *receive* testimony. That distinction — extract vs. receive — is the book's entire argument. The subtitle should dramatize that tension, not just describe the plot.

The existing subtitle in the bible is:
> *A Novel: A Harvard Archaeologist Reads a 2,000-Year-Old Witness to the Crucifixion—and Must Choose Between Data That Makes Her Career and Testimony That Remakes Her*

That subtitle is strong. It names the protagonist's credential (Harvard Archaeologist), the object (2,000-Year-Old Witness to the Crucifixion), and the stakes (career vs. soul). It is approximately 34 words, within KDP's character limit. It front-loads the academic hook for the skeptic and closes on the spiritual stakes for the believer.

## Subtitle Candidates

Offered in order of recommendation.

---

### CANDIDATE A — Recommended
**A Harvard Archaeologist, a 2,000-Year-Old Witness to the Crucifixion, and the Choice Between the Career That Proves It and the Faith It Demands**

*Why it works:* "Witness" does double duty — it names what the tree is (an eyewitness) and carries the legal and theological resonance the word holds throughout the book. "The choice between the career that proves it and the faith it demands" encodes the extract/receive tension in reader-friendly language: proving something is the scientist's act; faith demanded of you is the receiver's act. Slightly more lyrical than Candidate B without sacrificing search value.

*Character count (approx):* ~160

*Keyword value:* Harvard Archaeologist, Crucifixion — strong. Faith — moderate. Historical implied.

---

### CANDIDATE B — Close Second
**A Harvard Archaeologist Uncovers a 2,000-Year-Old Witness to the Crucifixion — and Must Choose Between the Data That Saves Her Career and the Testimony That Changes Everything**

*Why it works:* "Testimony That Changes Everything" is familiar to the Christian fiction reader — it echoes conversion and witness culture. "Data That Saves Her Career" makes the cost concrete. The parallel structure is clean and easy to scan.

*Character count (approx):* ~158

*Keyword value:* Harvard Archaeologist, Crucifixion, Testimony — strong.

---

### CANDIDATE C — Suspense-Forward
**When a Harvard Archaeologist Reads the Cross Itself, She Must Decide Whether the Evidence Is a Career-Making Discovery — or a Two-Thousand-Year-Old Call She Can't Refuse**

*Why it works:* "The Cross Itself" lands faster than "a 2,000-Year-Old Witness" for readers who don't want to parse. "A Call She Can't Refuse" borrows thriller energy without losing spiritual meaning — the call is scientific, professional, and theological simultaneously. Small risk: "Can't Refuse" has a faint idiom echo, though at this character count it reads as natural, not cinematic.

*Character count (approx):* ~170

*Keyword value:* Harvard Archaeologist, Cross — moderate (less specific than Crucifixion).

---

### CANDIDATE D — Plainspoken (also strong as a tagline)
**A Harvard Archaeologist. A 2,000-Year-Old Witness to the Crucifixion. One Choice: Publish the Data — or Receive the Testimony**

*Why it works:* The fragment structure creates rhythm and white space on the page. "Publish the Data — or Receive the Testimony" is the book's argument in six words. Very scannable. Risk: it may read as thin on mobile covers where subtitle text is small. Strong alternative for back cover tagline or author bio header.

*Character count (approx):* ~125

*Keyword value:* Harvard Archaeologist, Crucifixion, Testimony — strong.

---

## Recommendation

**Use Candidate A** as the primary option to evaluate against the existing subtitle.

The existing subtitle in the bible is genuinely good and should not be changed lightly — it has been in place through the full revision process. The main recommendation is to consider Candidates A and D if KDP metadata is being refreshed. Candidate D is the strongest for any tagline use (back cover, author bio header, series page).

The current subtitle ("...Data That Makes Her Career and Testimony That Remakes Her") is rhythmically the best of all candidates. The only argument for changing it is that "Remakes Her" reads slightly softer than "Demands" or "Changes Everything" for readers scanning thriller categories. That is a marginal concern.

---

## Final Note on Keyword Placement

KDP's subtitle field is indexed for search. The words "Harvard Archaeologist," "Crucifixion," "Witness," "Testimony," and "Faith" are all doing search work in addition to their rhetorical work. Any subtitle revision should preserve at minimum: **Harvard Archaeologist** + **Crucifixion** + one of {Testimony / Witness / Faith}.
