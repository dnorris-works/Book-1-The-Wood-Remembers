# Chapter 40: The Decision

## Personal Conviction

*February 18, 2025 - St. Paul Catholic Church, Cambridge*

Sarah knelt in the back pew of St. Paul's, staring at the crucifix above the altar. Two weeks of morning visits, and still she wasn't ready to receive Communion—the gap between intellectual belief and spiritual surrender too wide to bridge with good intentions alone.

The Gothic architecture reminded her of Notre Dame's Basilica, where she'd attended daily Mass as an undergraduate. Back then, faith had seemed as natural as breathing. Now it felt like learning to breathe underwater.

Until Jerusalem. Until the fragments had challenged every materialist assumption she'd built her career upon.

"Struggling with something?"

She turned to see an elderly priest settling into the pew beside her, his movements careful but determined. His name tag read "Fr. Murphy."

"Is it that obvious?"

"You've been sitting in the same spot every morning for two weeks, staring at the crucifix like you're waiting for it to speak." A pause. "What do you see when you look at it?"

Sarah followed his gaze to the simple wooden cross above the altar. She thought about the fragments. About two thousand years of hands choosing preservation over safety. About a monk who had written his complaint into the custody record and kept faith anyway.

"I see wood that held something I don't have a word for yet," she said.

Father Murphy nodded, as if this were a reasonable answer. "That's probably where it starts," he said. He stood, nodded once, and left her to it.

## Institutional Response

After Father Murphy left for his next appointment, Sarah remained in the pew, surrounded by the silence that only old churches could hold. Her phone buzzed with a text from her cousin Maria: "*Babcia's* novena candles are still burning for you. She always knew this day would come."

That afternoon, Sarah called Father Kowalski in Jerusalem. The international connection crackled slightly, but his voice came through clear.

"Michael, I need to ask you something directly. Is this Vatican position real? Or is it a consolation prize because the Church feels guilty about what Harvard's putting me through?"

"Sarah, Cardinal Bertoli specifically requested you for this position based on your work with MEMORIA. The Pontifical Academy considers quantum archaeology the future of biblical archaeology. They want the person who pioneered the field to lead their research program."

"And if the MEMORIA findings don't hold up under broader scrutiny?"

"Then you'll be the world's leading expert in quantum archaeological methodology. Win-win."

Sarah was quiet for a long moment, watching snow fall outside her apartment window. "Michael, can I ask you something personal?"

"Of course."

"Do you really believe consciousness can be preserved in wood for two thousand years?"

"Sarah, I believe God became human and died on a cross to save the world. After that, consciousness preserved in wood seems almost mundane."

His matter-of-fact tone made her laugh—the first genuine laugh she'd had in weeks. "When you put it that way..."

"The question isn't whether it's possible. The question is whether you're brave enough to follow the evidence wherever it leads, even when it leads away from everything you thought you wanted."

## Scientific Validation

After the call ended, Sarah sat at her kitchen table with two draft emails on her laptop screen. The first was to Dr. Harper, formally declining to distance herself from her Jerusalem findings and accepting whatever consequences that decision entailed. The second was to Cardinal Bertoli, accepting the Vatican position and requesting transfer of all research materials to Rome.

Before sending them, she called her grandmother's old number—still active, now answered by her cousin Maria who maintained the apartment exactly as *Babcia* had left it.

"Maria, it's Sarah. Can you do something for me? Can you light a candle at *Babcia's* prayer shrine and ask her to pray for me? I'm about to make a decision that would have made her very proud and my Harvard professors very angry."

"Sarah," Maria said gently, "*Babcia's* been praying for this moment for seventy years. Light your own candle. She'll hear you."

Sarah hung up and walked to her kitchen window, looking out at the Cambridge lights twinkling in the February darkness. Somewhere in Rome, cardinals were debating how to present her findings to the world. Somewhere in Jerusalem, limestone caves held secrets that quantum computers could detect but human hearts had always known.

She thought about her father's letter, still folded in her wallet: *God doesn't need our certainty. He needs our faithfulness in the darkness.*

It was time to choose faithfulness.

## Public Announcement

She sent both emails with swift clicks that felt like stepping off a cliff. Then she knelt beside her coffee table where her grandmother's rosary lay next to her Harvard faculty ID—two symbols of different worlds that had shaped her but could no longer contain her.

She picked up the rosary and began to pray, the first time in years that prayer felt less like petition and more like homecoming. The Hail Mary came back to her in Polish, the way *Babcia* had taught her, the words carrying the weight of seventy years of immigrant faith that had somehow survived Harvard skepticism and academic pressure to bloom again in a Cambridge apartment at the moment when choosing faith meant choosing everything that mattered most.

"*Zdrowaś Maryjo, łaski pełna, Pan z Tobą...*"

The familiar rhythm of the prayer connected her across time and space to her grandmother's kitchen in South Bend, where a little girl had learned that some truths were too large for understanding but not too large for trust.

Outside, Cambridge slept peacefully, unaware that a decision made in a small apartment might determine how the world understood the relationship between faith and reason for generations to come. Inside, a scholar was becoming something more than a scholar—not less rigorous, but more faithful; not less intelligent, but more trusting; not less human, but more willing to let mystery be mystery while still pursuing truth with everything she had.

Her phone chimed with the first response—an automated receipt from Cardinal Bertoli's office: "Thank you for your communication. His Eminence will respond personally within 24 hours."

Then another chime—Dr. Harper's reply: "Sarah, I'm sorry to hear of your decision. Your final paperwork will be processed within the week. We wish you well in your future endeavors."

No anger, no recrimination. Just the careful neutrality of an institution protecting itself from association with someone who had chosen truth over convenience.

Sarah smiled, feeling lighter than she had in months. She was unemployed, professionally controversial, and facing an uncertain future in a foreign country. But for the first time since Jerusalem, she felt like herself again.

The decision was made. The real journey was just beginning.

## After the Leak

*Early March, 2025 — Cambridge*

The apartment was a different apartment by then — same walls, same window, but the air in it had changed. Rome had receded into the flight she hadn't yet booked. The *Skeptical Inquiry* piece had run. The security report had been filed. Webb had been in her lab for three years and would not be in her lab again.

Her phone rang.

She looked at the screen. Marcus Webb. She let it ring twice, then answered.

"I heard you filed the report with university security." His voice was flat. Not frightened. Flat in the way of someone who has prepared what they're going to say.

"I did."

"I want you to know I don't regret it." A pause. "I want you to know I did it because someone had to."

Sarah sat down on the edge of the couch. "Come over."

Another pause, longer. "Excuse me?"

"You want to tell me why. Come tell me why."

He arrived twenty minutes later, still in his coat, his face carrying the specific tightness of someone who has rehearsed a conversation and is now uncertain the rehearsal will hold. He was thirty-one years old, she remembered. She had written his fellowship recommendation eighteen months ago. He had been good then. He was still good, in the narrow sense of the word.

"Sit down," she said.

He sat at the kitchen table. She stayed standing.

"You had access to my files for three years," she said. "You knew what this work meant to me. You knew what the peer review process was. You went around it."

"Because peer review wouldn't have stopped you." He said it without apology. "You would have published, the Vatican would have amplified it, and five years from now high school science teachers would be explaining how Catholic archaeologists proved consciousness survives death. That's what I was stopping."

"You don't know what the data shows."

"I know what you think it shows. That's the problem." He leaned forward slightly. "Sarah, you're brilliant. You built something genuinely new. And then you pointed it at ancient wood and saw God, and you—" He stopped. Started again. "You're going to spend the rest of your career legitimizing bad epistemology because you want your grandmother's faith to be true. I've watched it happen. I'm not going to let it happen on my watch without doing something."

Sarah looked at him for a long moment.

He wasn't lying. That was the thing she'd understood from the report, from the careful pattern of his access logs, from the methodical way he'd chosen *Skeptical Inquiry* over tabloids, real physicists over pundits. He had not acted out of jealousy alone, though the jealousy was real. He had acted because he genuinely believed he was right. He had looked at her work and decided it was dangerous and decided that destroying it was a form of care.

He didn't know what he had done. Not in the way that mattered.

He had copied her files and handed them to people who would use them as ammunition, and he had done it convinced he was protecting something worth protecting. He was wrong about almost everything that mattered. He didn't know what the data was detecting. He didn't know what he was trying to erase. He had stood over something he couldn't see and swung anyway, certain in the dark.

She thought of a sentence she hadn't said aloud in years, because she'd stopped believing sentences like it meant anything. It surfaced now without her choosing it, the way old knowledge surfaces when something in you recognizes its use.

She didn't say it. She just held it.

"I'm dropping the charges," she said.

His expression shifted. Not relief — something closer to confusion. "Why."

"Because you didn't know what you were doing." She said it simply. Not as absolution. Just as the fact it was.

He stared at her. "I knew exactly what I was doing."

"You knew what you thought you were doing. That's not the same thing." She picked up her grandmother's rosary from the coffee table, not to use it, just to hold it. The beads were cold. "You tried to stop me from corrupting science. But the data is real, Marcus. Whatever you believe about what it means, the signal is real. Chen confirmed it. You handed my methodology to people who can't find the error because there isn't one." She set the rosary down. "You didn't know that when you sent the files. So I'm dropping the charges."

He was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had lost its preparation. "You're going to Rome."

"Yes."

"And you think this is right."

"I think the data is real and I'm going to follow it." She met his eyes. "I think you should read Chen's preliminary co-author notes before you decide what you believe about the rest."

He stood. Put on his coat with the specific slowness of someone who doesn't know what to do with what's happened. At the door he turned back.

"I still think you're wrong," he said.

"I know you do."

He left. She heard his steps go down the stairs, heard the street door close.

The apartment was quiet again. She sat with what the quiet felt like — not clean, not resolved. Something had been given up that she would not get back. The charges had been her right. Dropping them cost exactly what she had given: the satisfaction of being right about what he deserved.

That was what it cost. That was all it cost. She sat with that for a minute, then stood up.

She closed the laptop. Cambridge was dark outside the window. Whatever came next would come from Rome.
