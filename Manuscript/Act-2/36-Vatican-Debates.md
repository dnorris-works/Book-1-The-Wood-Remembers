# Chapter 36: Vatican Debates

*March 3–4, 2025 — Vatican Apostolic Palace, Rome*

## The Argument

## Evidence on the Table

The fragment sat between them on the mahogany table, small and dark under the Apostolic Palace's ornate ceiling frescoes. Eight centuries of Church history had soaked into this wood—the table's, not the fragment's. Though if Dr. Davidson's findings proved accurate, the fragment had witnessed far more.

Cardinal Heinrich Ostermann's fingers drummed against the armrest. Once. Twice. Three times. The German precision that had made him legendary during his years at the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith translated into rhythmic impatience.

"This is madness, Alessandro."

Cardinal Alessandro Bertoli said nothing. He reached across the table and picked up the fragment, holding it between them like evidence at trial. The wood felt warm against his palm—warmer than room temperature should allow after resting in climate-controlled storage.

"We're talking about endorsing claims that overturn fundamental physics," Ostermann continued. His fingers kept drumming. "Quantum coherence in biological systems for geological timescales? Consciousness preserved in wood? This isn't archaeology—it's fantasy dressed in laboratory jargon."

"The MEMORIA data—"

"The MEMORIA data could be instrument artifacts. Dr. Davidson is a recent convert undergoing religious enthusiasm. Her colleague Dr. Chen has three months of Vatican funding. Neither has incentive toward skepticism."

Cardinal Luis Tagle's voice came through the video screen, gentle but firm. "Heinrich, the preliminary findings have been reviewed by our own physicists. They found the methodology sound."

"Sound methodology can still produce unsound conclusions." Ostermann's fingers stopped drumming. He leaned forward. "Alessandro, if we authenticate these fragments, we face demands to authenticate every relic claim across six continents. We risk reducing mystery to laboratory analysis. The resurrection doesn't require archaeological proof."

Bertoli held the fragment up to the light streaming through tall windows. The wood's grain showed dark striations—old blood, perhaps, or mineral staining from fourteen centuries in limestone darkness. His palm registered the warmth more clearly now. Not dramatic. Not miraculous. Just... present.

"Heinrich," he said quietly, "what if God chose to leave evidence? What if incarnational theology means matter can bear witness to divine touch?"

"Then God has poor understanding of epistemology." Ostermann's voice carried the edge that had terrified heretics for decades. "Physical evidence invites physical scrutiny. We open ourselves to skeptics with better equipment proving our 'miraculous' fragments are medieval forgeries preserved through wishful thinking."

"Or proving them authentic."

"At what cost? Catholic credibility rests on faith transcending evidence. We become another religion scrambling for proof when proof isn't the point."

Cardinal Robert Sarah spoke for the first time, his voice carrying weight of someone who'd watched Western intellectual fashion come and go while serving the Church across three continents. "Perhaps the question isn't whether evidence exists. The question is whether we have courage to investigate it honestly."

The fragment was definitely warming in Bertoli's hand now. He could feel heat spreading from the wood into his palm, up his wrist. Probably just thermal transfer from his own body. Probably just blood flow responding to the stress of this conversation.

Probably.

"Father Kowalski's report documents quantum signatures that exceed theoretical limits by trillion-fold margins," Bertoli said. "Dr. Chen—who came here to debunk Davidson's research—now admits the data defies conventional explanation. These aren't enthusiasts seeing what they want to see. These are skeptics confronting implications they'd prefer to dismiss."

"Implications that sound like mysticism masquerading as science."

"Perhaps mysticism was describing science we didn't have mathematics for yet."

Ostermann's fingers started drumming again. Faster now. "You're asking us to stake Church credibility on wood fragments and quantum physics. On claims that consciousness can be preserved through love's intensity. On—"

"On honesty," Bertoli said.

The drumming stopped.

"The Church that venerates these fragments is descended from both the people who preserved them and the authorities who demanded the execution that created them." Bertoli set the fragment on the table between them. "Prisca hid pieces of this wood in the catacombs while Rome was still burning Christians. Caiaphas signed the order that made the wood necessary. We are both of their heirs. If we suppress evidence because it is inconvenient, we are acting in the tradition of one and not the other. That is what I am asking you to consider."

The room was quiet.

Ostermann looked at the fragment for a long moment. Then he reached across the table and picked it up himself. Turned it in his hands. Set it back down.

Whatever he had felt or not felt, he kept to himself.

"The Singapore replication," he said. "Independent team. No knowledge of the original protocol parameters."

"Confirmed the coherence patterns. Yes."

Ostermann's hands went flat on the table. "I want it on record that I retain significant doubts about the theoretical framework. The quantum mechanics as proposed has not been adequately explained."

"Noted."

"And that I believe the institutional risks are real and should not be minimized."

"Also noted."

Cardinal Sarah leaned forward. "Is there a vote in there, Heinrich?"

Ostermann looked at the fragment once more. Something moved through his expression that Bertoli couldn't fully read — not conversion, and not capitulation. Something more like a man who had been given an argument he couldn't answer and had decided not to pretend otherwise.

"We vote tomorrow," he said. "After we've each spent tonight in prayer rather than debate."

Bertoli's palm still felt warm where the fragment had rested.

"Agreed," he said.

## The Cardinal's Night

Two A.M. in the Chapel

The wooden crucifix felt heavy in Bertoli's hands.

His grandmother had given it to him at ordination—simple olive wood from the Holy Land, carved by Palestinian craftsmen, blessed by a parish priest rather than papal authority. Nothing ornate. Nothing theologically significant. Just plain wood shaped for devotion.

He'd carried it through thirty years of Vatican service, through diplomatic postings and curial politics and the slow climb toward cardinal's red. The crucifix had witnessed his transformation from ambitious priest to something approaching actual shepherd. It knew his prayers when no one else was listening.

Now it felt like an accusation.

Bertoli sat alone in his private chapel at 2 AM, unable to sleep, unable to stop thinking about the fragment that had glowed in his hand six hours ago. The Vatican statement sat half-drafted on his laptop, cursor blinking in the darkness lit only by the sanctuary lamp's perpetual flame.

"The authenticated fragments represent tangible witness to divine love's triumph over—"

He deleted the sentence for the third time.

Over what? Institutional failure? Human sinfulness? The religious authorities who'd demanded Christ's execution to preserve Temple stability?

The same kind of religious authorities Bertoli had become.

He looked at the crucifix in his hands. Looked at the laptop screen. Looked at the sanctuary lamp burning before the tabernacle where Christ's presence was maintained through institutional sacrament.

The wood that Dr. Davidson had found—that had glowed in his palm—had witnessed Caiaphas. The high priest who'd served God by killing God. Religious authority so blinded by institutional preservation that it couldn't recognize the Holy One standing trial in its courts.

And now Bertoli, another religious authority descended from that same institutional lineage, was writing official Vatican statements about that wood.

The irony tasted like ashes.

His phone sat silent on the prie-dieu. He could call Father Kowalski in Jerusalem, ask for theological guidance, receive the Jesuit's measured wisdom about divine providence working through institutional failure.

But he knew what Michael would say. Knew because they'd discussed it over espresso in Vatican gardens two weeks ago, that conversation that had started this whole discernment process.

"The fragments exist to remind us that Christ died for religious authorities too. Even the ones who crucified Him."

Bertoli set down the crucifix and returned to his laptop. Started typing, fingers finding words that honesty demanded:

"The authenticated fragments bear witness to divine love that triumphed not only over death but over the institutional failures—including religious institutional failures—that made crucifixion necessary. The Church that venerates these fragments must acknowledge we are descended from both the faithful who preserved them and the authorities who demanded the execution that created them."

He paused. Read it back. The words sounded right but felt incomplete.

What was he really trying to say? That the Vatican apologized for Caiaphas? That modern cardinals bore guilt for first-century high priests? That institutional Christianity somehow needed to atone for pre-Christian Temple authorities?

No. Something else. Something harder.

His fingers moved across the keyboard:

"This dual inheritance requires humility rather than triumphalism. We validate these fragments not from position of authority vindicated but from position of authority chastened. The wood witnessed both sin and redemption. Both conspiracy and conversion. Both rejection and recognition."

Better. Closer to the truth that was keeping him awake.

He looked at his grandmother's crucifix again. Remembered her words when she'd given it to him: "Carry this and remember—the Church exists to serve the Cross, not the other way around. When we forget that, we become Caiaphas."

Bertoli's throat tightened. His grandmother had been simple woman, working-class piety without theological sophistication. But she'd understood something that decades of Vatican service had almost made him forget.

The Cross judged the Church. Not the other way around.

He typed the final sentence:

"The wood forgives what we cannot. Perhaps that is its greatest witness."

Outside his chapel windows, Roman church bells announced 2:30 AM—the hour when Christ had prayed in Gethsemane while institutional authorities plotted His arrest. The hour when love had chosen suffering over escape, when divinity had submitted to human injustice to redeem that injustice from within.

Bertoli saved the draft and knelt before the tabernacle, holding his grandmother's simple crucifix, praying for wisdom that exceeded institutional calculation.

The wood in his hands felt warm.

## The Vote

Silence Speaks

Four cardinals around the table. One on video screen. The fragment resting between them under morning light that made its surface look almost alive.

Nobody had spoken for three minutes.

Cardinal Ostermann sat with his hands folded, fingers finally still after yesterday's drumming. His eyes kept returning to the fragment, then away, then back. As if he didn't trust what he'd seen but couldn't deny the seeing.

Cardinal Ouellet studied his notes without reading them. Cardinal Sarah prayed silently, lips moving soundlessly through what looked like the Jesus Prayer. Cardinal Tagle watched from Manila via secure connection, his face unreadable.

Bertoli had read his statement aloud ten minutes ago. The words about institutional failure, about dual inheritance, about wood forgiving what they couldn't. The silence that followed had weight.

Finally, Ostermann spoke: "You're asking us to admit the Church descends from both preservers and destroyers. From both saints and conspirators."

"I'm asking us to tell the truth."

"The full truth?"

"The full truth."

Ostermann looked at the fragment again. "Last night, I prayed before that fragment for three hours. Asking God whether what I'd seen was real. Whether the glow was Divine sign or elaborate deception."

"And?" Cardinal Sarah asked quietly.

"And I received no answer. Just silence. Just the fragment resting in my hands, ordinary wood containing extraordinary implications." Ostermann met Bertoli's eyes. "I still have doubts, Alessandro. About the physics. About whether consciousness can truly be preserved through quantum mechanisms. About whether we're wise to make pronouncements on evidence this unprecedented."

"Then vote your doubts."

"I can't." Ostermann's voice dropped to whisper. "Because I also have faith. And faith admits that God's creativity might exceed my physics. That matter touched by infinite love might bear traces of that encounter through means my theology hasn't categorized yet."

He reached out and touched the fragment with one finger. Withdrew his hand quickly.

"I abstain."

The word hung in the air like incense. Not rejection. Not endorsement. Just honest acknowledgment of mystery that exceeded certainty.

Cardinal Ouellet nodded slowly. "I vote to proceed. With full scientific rigor. With complete theological scrutiny. But to proceed."

"As do I," Cardinal Sarah said.

"And I," Cardinal Tagle added from the screen.

All eyes turned to Bertoli.

He looked at each of them in turn. At Ostermann's abstention bought with three hours of prayer and intellectual honesty. At Ouellet's measured support. At Sarah's faith that could hold both doubt and belief simultaneously. At Tagle's Asian wisdom that knew some truths required patience rather than certainty.

"Gentlemen," he said quietly, "we proceed. Not because we have proof. Not because we've eliminated every doubt. But because truth serves truth, and these fragments deserve investigation matching both their scientific significance and their spiritual implications."

He picked up the fragment one more time. It was warm again—or maybe his imagination supplied warmth because hope demanded it.

"Truth serves truth," Cardinal Ostermann echoed. His first agreement all morning.

The vote was recorded. The decision made. The investigation that would either revolutionize Catholic archaeology or mark them all as fools susceptible to mystical delusion would continue under official Vatican authority.

Nobody celebrated. Nobody spoke. They simply sat in silence while morning light through palace windows painted the fragment's surface in shades of amber and gold.

Outside, Rome continued its eternal rhythm—pilgrims and tourists, politicians and priests, the city that had witnessed two millennia of Church history making room for one more unprecedented moment.

Inside, five cardinals had chosen to investigate rather than dismiss, to proceed rather than protect, to trust that wood touched by divine love might speak across centuries through means they didn't yet comprehend.

The fragment lay between them in the morning light, ordinary and warm.

Nobody reached for it.
