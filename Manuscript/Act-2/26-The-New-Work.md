# Chapter 26: The New Work

## The Caelian Hill

*313 CE — Six Months After the Edict — Church of San Clemente, Rome*

The men came on a Thursday, when the congregation was at evening prayer.

Marcus was not supposed to be there. His patrol ended at the Via Sacra, and the church on the Caelian Hill was technically in Quintus's district. But Quintus was down with a fever, and Marcus had found himself rerouting his evening walk without quite deciding to. He'd done it four times this week. He didn't examine the habit.

He heard them before he saw them. Two sets of footsteps moving with purpose, a third following — the sound of people who had agreed on something. He turned the corner and they were already at the door of the church: two men in the tunics of dock laborers, a third who dressed like a minor official and was pretending to be out for an evening stroll. The pretense was poor. The dock workers had the flat, appraising look of men hired for a specific task rather than men who lived in this neighborhood.

Inside, muffled by stone, the psalm continued.

*Miserere mei, Deus, secundum magnam misericordiam tuam.* Have mercy on me, O God, according to your great mercy.

The official saw Marcus first. His expression cycled through surprise, calculation, and something harder — not quite fear, the assessment of a man deciding how much trouble a soldier would be.

"Centurion." A tone that was meant to land as neutral and landed as wary.

"Evening." Marcus kept his hands loose at his sides, away from his gladius. He had learned something in six months: the sword was his old language. This district required a different one. "Something I can help you with?"

"Just walking." The official's eyes moved to the dock workers, who had stepped back from the door. "Nice evening for it."

"It is." Marcus looked at the church door, then back at the man. Not accusingly. Just — looking, the way a man looks when he sees exactly what is happening and both parties know it. "Warm for the season."

A pause in which all four of them understood perfectly what was occurring and no one said it. The psalm inside reached its next verse, a dozen voices woven together, audible enough through the wall to hear the grain of it — men and women, the particular sound of people who have been singing this for years.

*Quoniam iniquitatem meam ego cognosco, et peccatum meum contra me est semper.* I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me.

The official's eyes moved to the door again. Then back to Marcus.

"Constantine's edict is specific," Marcus said. He said it without threat, the way a man states the current weather. "These people are protected citizens. The edict carries the emperor's name. I imagine you know this."

"I'm not—"

"I know," Marcus said. "You're walking. Nice evening." He held the man's gaze long enough to make the point, not long enough to require a response. "I'll be along myself in a bit. Completing my patrol."

The official gave a short nod. He turned and walked, and the dock workers followed without needing to be told. Their footsteps receded down the hill toward the warehouse district, and Marcus stood listening until he could no longer hear them.

He stood for a while after.

The psalm inside had moved on to its sixth verse. He knew the words now — Tertius had been patient with a soldier who had not sung since childhood. *Ecce enim in iniquitatibus conceptus sum, et in peccato concepit me mater mea.* Behold, I was brought forth in iniquity. The honesty of it still surprised him. No evasion in these prayers. No diplomatic management of what was true. Just the full weight of the thing laid out in plain language and offered upward.

He had found this, in six months, to be the most disorienting part of his conversion. Not the theology — Tertius had walked him through the theology with the patience of a man accustomed to teaching difficult students, and the theology had its own logic that Marcus could follow. What he had not expected was the prayers. What the psalms did to a man who had been trained his whole life to project certainty. What it meant to kneel and say: this is what I am. All of it.

The door opened. Tertius.

He took in the empty street, Marcus, the direction the footsteps had gone. His face held a particular kind of recognition — not relief exactly. The recognition of a man who has been afraid for decades and knows the difference between a night when something happened and a night when something was prevented.

"You were here," he said.

"Completing my patrol."

Tertius looked at him for a moment. Then, quietly: "Thank you, Marcus."

The two words cost him something, Marcus could see that. This man whose twin he had killed was looking at him and saying *thank you* and meaning it, and the grace required for that was not something Marcus had yet found a way to think about without his chest tightening.

"The emperor's edict—" Marcus started.

"I know what you're going to say." Tertius almost smiled. "You're going to say you were only doing your assigned duty."

"I was."

"I know." He pushed the door open wider. "Come in for evening prayer. Or stand there in the street. Either is fine."

Marcus stood in the street for another moment, aware of his gladius at his hip, his soldier's instinct still cataloguing the neighborhood — exits, sightlines, the quality of the light. Old habits don't go silent the way you want them to.

Then he went inside.

## What Changed and What Didn't

He kept the role for eleven years.

Not because the empire gave him no other option — his record was excellent, reassignment would have been possible. He asked to stay. Requested it twice, once when the district officer tried to rotate him out, once when a promotion would have moved him across the Tiber. Tertius raised an eyebrow at the second refusal, and Marcus told him: *I know this street. I know the faces. Someone else will have to learn it from the beginning, and there are people here who can't afford the learning period.*

Tertius had not pushed further. He had the tact of a man who understood that some conversions take years to complete, and that the completion looks different than the beginning, and that keeping vigil over a church on the Caelian Hill is not the worst form a life can take.

The nights were mostly uneventful. That was the truth Marcus had not expected: the work was mostly nothing. It was walking in the dark past a door that remained closed. Patrols that ended without incident. The same faces, the same streets, the same sounds of evening prayer that he had come to know by season — different psalms in different months, the congregation's voices shifting slightly year to year as members died and children grew old enough to join the singing.

What he had expected, in the first months, was that the protection would feel heroic. A continuation of the conversion moment, the same quality of before-and-after. Something he could point to and say: this is the new thing I am.

What it actually felt like was maintenance.

Going out in cold weather to stand at a corner. Recognizing the same minor official from the Thursday incident, four months later, in a different part of the district, and making eye contact long enough to be understood. Sitting with Tertius in the empty church on a winter morning while frost ran the windows and the question of whether anything had happened in the grain of very old wood seemed, in the gray light, both important and completely unanswerable.

"Do you believe it now?" Marcus asked once. *The fragments. Do you believe what I felt when I held them?*

Tertius was quiet for a long time. "I believe Stephanos did. I believe he held them when he was a student and said they made him want to copy the Gospels, and that he spent his life doing that." He looked at the cedar box behind the altar. "Whether what you felt was what was in the wood or what was in you, I don't know. I'm not sure the distinction matters."

"It matters to me."

"Yes." Tertius looked at him with something that might have been affection, or something adjacent to it — the particular regard of a man who has survived something terrible in company with someone he didn't expect. "That's what makes you worth arguing with."

They argued carefully, for eleven years. Not bitterly. The way two people argue when they're working toward something neither of them can reach alone.

Marcus gave the fragments to a deacon named Eustace when the governor's rotation finally came through and he had no legitimate reason to refuse it. He was fifty-one years old. His sword hand still worked. He did not consider himself healed — that was not the right word, would never be the right word for the specific history of those hands. What he considered himself was *employed differently*. A description that was accurate without flattering. He had found he preferred accurate.

He converted formally in his forty-fourth year, three years later than Tertius had expected and eight years sooner than Marcus himself had. The cold water on his forehead, the old priest's words, the specific way the small congregation on the Caelian Hill made room for him in the benches — all of it simpler than he'd imagined. Less transformation, more arrival.

*I kept faith,* Matthias would write three hundred years later, in a cistern, of a different fragment, in a city he was afraid would not survive the night.

Marcus would not have written it that way. He would have said: *I kept watch.* The grammar of soldiers rather than monks. The accounting of someone who stood at corners in the dark and understood that the point of standing at corners in the dark is that nothing happens.

What he carried, in those eleven years, was not the fragments — Tertius held those, in the cedar box behind the altar, where they belonged. What Marcus carried was the knowledge that the street outside was the street he had made dangerous once, and that making it safe was not an erasure of that fact but was, perhaps, the only response available to a man who could not undo what his hands had done.

He held Eustace's shoulders when he passed the custody on. He was aware, as he did, of the stiffness in his right arm — an old wound from a campaign thirty years back — and of Eustace's hands, young and steady, a deacon's hands that had not yet held everything they would hold.

*Go carefully,* he said. Not orders. Advice from one custodian to another.

Eustace nodded, which was enough.

*Six months after he held me in the church where he wept on the floor, Marcus stood at a street corner on the Caelian Hill and did nothing, which was the whole of what was needed.*

*I was behind the altar by then. I felt none of it directly. But I know what happened the way I know the complete record of what has touched me and what has moved in my vicinity across two thousand years — in the way that matter holds what it has been near.*

*The three men left. The psalm continued.*

*I had felt the grip of his hands when he held me — fierce beneath the trembling, a man discovering what he would not release. That was the conversion. What came after was less dramatic and more difficult: eleven years of ordinary work done carefully, in weather, without a moment equal to the one that had changed him.*

*There is a kind of faith that lives in the conversion. The moment of breaking, the extraordinary weight of it, the sense that something has been permanently rearranged.*

*There is another kind that lives in the maintenance. The patrol that ends without incident. The vigil that asks nothing of you except that you show up again.*

*Marcus had both. He would have said, if asked, that the second kind was harder.*

*He was not wrong.*

*I do not carry his name in any record. He wrote nothing. What I carry is the quality of his hands — the fierce grip beneath the trembling, the decade-long steadiness that followed it. He was not a great man in the way that word usually applies. He was a man who discovered what he would not release and spent eleven years releasing nothing.*

*That is enough. For wood, that is enough.*
