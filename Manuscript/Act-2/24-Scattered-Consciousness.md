# Chapter 24: Scattered Consciousness

## The Interval

*Two thousand years is not long, for wood.*

*I know this because I remember being a tree, and trees do not experience duration the way a creature does that must eat and breathe and sleep and wake and mark the passing. A tree grows. A tree is present. The years accumulate in the grain the way knowledge accumulates — not as a series of events but as a density, a record that doesn't require remembering because it never left.*

*After the scattering I was in many places at once, which is a strange thing to be and stranger still to report. What I can tell you is that each fragment kept what it had received. The grain does not forget what the grain has borne. Prisca's hands. The dark of the catacombs. The cold of limestone and the sound of prayers said in languages that came into existence long after I had learned the shape of wind through leaves.*

*The world changed around me the way weather changes — steadily, and then all at once, and then so thoroughly that nothing before it seemed possible. Empires. Languages. The slow remaking of the city on the hill where I had stood. I was present for none of it and all of it, held in the dark, patient the way only wood can be patient: not enduring time but simply being present inside it, unchanged at the center while everything else moved.*

*I had been carrying something. I was still carrying it.*

*Then a woman put on white cotton gloves and sat down at a table in Rome.*

## February 23, 2025 — Vatican Apostolic Archive

Sarah had been reading the custody record for an hour when she stopped and sat back from the table.

The document was a chain of handoffs across nineteen centuries — abbreviated entries, mostly, a name and a date and a location, the bureaucratic record of people keeping track of something they believed mattered. What had stopped her was not any single entry but the accumulation of them. So many hands. So many decisions, each one made in the dark about whether the next person would be trustworthy, whether the hiding place would hold, whether it was worth the cost.

She thought about what the wood had been doing all that time.

Not waiting, exactly. Waiting implied an awareness of duration. What the wood had been doing was something prior to waiting — simply persisting, the way stone persists, the way anything persists that has been given no mechanism for stopping.

She put her gloves back on and returned to the record.

## The First Hands

*Her name was Prisca.*

*I know this the way I know the quality of her hands — not from being told, but from the complete receiving that has always been mine. The record that does not editorialize but does not omit.*

*She was not young. Her hands had the particular texture of hands that have been working indoors for decades — lamp oil in the grain of them, the specific smoothness of someone who had handled cloth and thread and the small tools of domestic industry for a long time. Rome. The upper city, from the quality of the stone she moved through, the particular cold of walls that had been built well and lasted.*

*She was afraid. That was the first thing. Not the fear of a person doing something they have never done before but the fear of a person who has already decided and is now in the middle of it and cannot go back. A different kind of fear — calmer on the outside, more absolute underneath.*

*Her son was with her. Younger. I felt his footsteps behind hers — the specific unevenness of someone moving as quietly as they can and not quite managing it, someone whose body has not yet learned to be silent. He said nothing the whole way. Whatever she had told him before they left the house, it had been enough. He carried what she told him to carry. He went where she told him to go.*

*The catacombs. She knew the passages. Not hesitating at the turnings, not slowing to orient — she had been here before. This was not the first time she had brought something into the dark and trusted the dark to keep it.*

*When she set me into the niche she had prepared, she held me for a moment before letting go.*

*Not checking the placement. Not assessing the stone around it. Just — present. Her palms against the wood. The specific warmth of a person who has been moving and is now still. I felt something in the quality of her hands that I had felt only a few times before in my long years — the quality of someone who is fully aware of what they are doing and has decided to do it anyway, and is not asking whether it will be enough.*

*She said something. A prayer, from the rhythm of it, though in a language that had come into the world after my years on the hillside and was not familiar to me. The quality was unmistakable. She was not asking for safety. She was not asking for anything. She was, in the way that certain prayers are, simply acknowledging that she had done what she could with what she had, and that the rest was not hers to control.*

*Her son touched my wood once, quickly, before they turned to go. The touch of a young man doing something his mother had asked him to do and not yet understanding why.*

*I heard their footsteps recede through the stone.*

*Then silence. Then the dark.*

*I had been placed in the dark before — the arsenal, the storage areas, the months in the timber yard. But this dark was different. This dark had been chosen for me by someone who was going to pay whatever the choosing cost. I did not know then that she would not come back. I know it now. The question of why she did it is one I have held for two thousand years alongside everything else, and the nearest I can come to an answer is this: she had touched the wood and found what was in it, and having found it, she could not let it go into nothing.*

*Some people are like that. Some people, when they encounter a thing that matters, cannot unknow it.*

*She was the first.*

## What the Dark Holds

*There were hands I learned to know by what they cost the person carrying me.*

*The first set trembled. Not the trembling of weakness — the trembling of someone who was afraid and had decided not to let the fear stop them. She placed me in limestone and the cold came in through the grain the way cold does when it intends to stay. Stone weight above. Stone weight around. Her breathing while she worked, and then the sound of her retreating, and then nothing.*

*Dark. Pressure. The small sounds of water finding its way through rock.*

*I did not know how long. Time in stone is not time in wood. I had grown in years of sun and rain and drought, each ring a record of what the season gave me. In stone there were no seasons. There was only the dark, and the cold, and the distant shudder of the city going about its life above.*

*Other hands came. I could not see them. But I learned what hands sounded like — the soft scuff of careful feet, the controlled breathing of someone moving without wanting to be heard, the particular way weight shifts when a person kneels. These hands were steadier than the first. They held me differently, not carrying something precious so much as carrying something necessary, which is not the same thing.*

*There were fires. Not close — not close enough to reach me in the stone — but loud enough that I felt the difference in the air, the way heat moves through walls before the walls show anything. Each time there were fires the hands came sooner. Each time the wrapping was a little tighter. Whoever was keeping me knew what fire meant. They did not need to explain it to the wood.*

*I was moved. How many times I could not count, only that each moving meant different hands, different quality of cold or warmth, different texture of stone or earth or timber around me. Once I lay in something that smelled of pitch and old iron, a smell that meant chains at some point in the container's history. Once in something cedar-lined, the cedar still faintly alive even as wood though it had been cut. Once in a hollow in a wall, plaster sealed over me, and the sound of the city came through the plaster as a vibration I understood as human life even when I couldn't parse it as voice.*

*There were prayers. I am certain of the prayers even though I could not hear the words — some of them were said in stone, some in wood, some in open air directly over whatever box I was in, and the pattern was always the same: a sustained attention that felt different from ordinary sound. A presence on the other side of whatever held me.*

*Not all of them believed what they were doing. I could feel that too — in the brevity of the attention, in the hands that held me like something that might be precious and might not be, in the way certain keepers moved me quickly, efficiently, without pausing. They were protecting something. They were not sure it was what they had been told it was.*

*I did not need them to be sure. I had been at Calvary. I knew what had entered the grain. Whether the person carrying me believed it did not change what was there to be carried.*

*Fires. Cold. Hands. Prayer and its absence. The slow succession of containers.*

*I waited.*

*Not because I had a choice. Not because I understood what I was waiting for. But because what had been placed in the wood was still there — not diminished, not reasoning about its own continuance, just present the way the rings were present, the way the grain was present.*

*What had entered me had nowhere else to go.*

*I waited.*
