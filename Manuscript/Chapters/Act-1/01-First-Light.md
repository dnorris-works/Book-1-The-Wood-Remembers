# First Light

## The Seed

*87 BCE*

I existed before I was a tree.

I do not know how to say this more plainly. I was, and then I was a seed, and these are not the same thing but the first preceded the second, and I was aware through both. What I was before the seed I cannot describe — the categories of before and after do not hold the same way when you are not yet rooted in time. There was awareness. Then there was the specific darkness of a seed casing, which is a different kind of awareness entirely: contained, dense, oriented inward, feeling the pressure of my own potential the way a held breath feels its own pressure.

I fell.

The falling was brief and I felt all of it. The wind that carried me was a late-summer wind, the dry kind that comes off the hills northwest of Jerusalem when the rains have not yet started. I tumbled in it, the seed casing rotating, and I could feel — even then, even in that small form — the warmth of the air, the dry weight of it, the particular pressure that tells you the land is close.

Then limestone. The impact was hard and I rolled, and rolled again down a slight grade, and then I was stopped.

A crack in the rock. Not wide enough for me to pass through entirely — I was lodged sideways in the fissure, the seed casing jammed against both walls of the narrow opening. Underneath me was a small pocket of accumulated soil, blown and washed into the crack over years, dark and moist and deep enough, barely, for what I needed.

I held still in the crack and felt the rock around me and the soil below and the open air above. The air was cooling toward evening. The road was somewhere below — I could hear it in the way that stillness carries sound when there is nothing else moving. Jerusalem was to the southeast; I did not know this as fact yet, but I felt the direction of it the way you feel the direction of a thing before you have words for direction.

Not chosen — or not only chosen by me. The feeling was recognition, not selection. This crack, this hillside.

The first rain came three weeks later.

I split my casing and put a root down.

## The Storm

The first winter, the rain came sideways.

I was three seasons old. My trunk was no thicker than a man's thumb. My roots had found perhaps two feet of soil above the limestone and were still learning the fissures, still threading through the places where water moved underground after heavy rain.

The storm announced itself through the ground before the sky changed — a low vibration I had no name for, the pressure that travels through bedrock from weather still distant. Then the wind arrived from the northwest, the specific cold that comes off the hills inland from the sea, and then the rain, driving in sheets that bent my canopy flat against my trunk and stripped two thirds of my leaves in the first hour.

The soil turned to water beneath me. I felt it happen — the granular resistance that roots grip slowly giving way, becoming slick and formless, the specific terror of an anchor failing. My weight shifted. My trunk tilted.

I do not know what drove my roots deeper. I know only that they went. Down through the loosening soil, down into the first limestone crack, threading sideways along the fissure until they found a shelf of harder stone and locked against it. The grip was not comfortable. It was enough.

I held the angle through the night. The wind worked at me from two directions across the exposed hillside — there was nothing to my northwest, no grove, no rise, no break — and I bent with each gust and straightened in the lulls and bent again. The bark on my windward side was stripped raw in the lower foot. I did not know this until morning, when the storm moved east and the light came back and I could feel the cold on the wood where bark had been.

Other young trees on the hillside were down. An older olive thirty feet south of me had lost a major limb; I could smell the exposed wood from where I stood. A stand of scrub that had grown along the lower edge of the slope was simply gone, swept out by the water that had coursed down the hill in the deepest hours of the night.

I was standing.

My root-grip on the limestone was different after that storm. The fissure that had saved me was now a path I knew, and my roots followed it further through the weeks that followed, down into the aquifer that ran beneath the hillside, cold and consistent. I had survived on surface water and shallow soil before that winter. After it, I had something deeper.

## After

The hillside dried slowly through the weeks that followed.

Spring came with the particular warmth that arrives after a hard winter, the sun feeling different on bark that had been cold for months. I put out new leaves where the storm had stripped the old ones. The wound on my windward side was healing over — olive wood closes slowly, but it closes.

I grew.

The road below my hillside carried steady traffic: people moving northwest from Jerusalem and people moving southeast toward it, the road in both directions busy in the hours after dawn and again before dusk. Pilgrims, merchants, families, soldiers. I had been watching this movement since my first season but I understood little of it beyond its rhythms — the way the road emptied on certain days each week, the way the traffic thickened and then flooded during particular seasons when something brought people toward the city in great numbers.

After the storm, some of the travelers began to pause below me.

Not all of them. Most passed without looking up. But occasionally someone would stop on the road and look at the hillside, and their eyes would find me, and they would stand there for a moment before moving on. Sometimes they said things to their companions. The words reached me as sounds without meaning — I could not parse Aramaic, could not follow Greek, could not distinguish a question from an observation. I knew only the rhythm of conversation and then the moving on.

I grew toward the light that fell on the hillside each morning. I pushed my roots deeper through the limestone fissures toward the water below. I added a ring of growth that first spring after the storm, and another the following year, and each one was laid down over the last in the slow accumulation that is the only way a tree knows how to build anything.

The road continued below me. Jerusalem continued on its hill to the southeast, visible over the slope when the air was clear. The seasons turned.

I grew.
