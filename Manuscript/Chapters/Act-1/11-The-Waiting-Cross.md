# The Waiting Cross

## The Yard

*33 CE - Passover Week*

There were other crosses in the yard.

Five of them, different sizes, different ages of wood. Two were older than me by the look of their surfaces — gray and checked at the ends where weather had worked the grain. One was newer, its wood still showing yellow heartwood at the cut surfaces, not yet fully cured. We stood together in the storage area in the posture of objects that have been made ready and are waiting for the use they were made for.

The yard was inside the city walls. I had never been inside the walls before. The sound was different: more enclosed, sound reflecting off stone rather than dissipating into open air. The smell was different too — stone dust and animal waste and the particular density of many thousands of people in close proximity, the smell that a city makes when it is full beyond its normal capacity.

Passover. I had heard the word on my hillside for over a hundred years, heard it with the anticipation that the pilgrims carried in their voices when they used it. Now I was inside the city during the thing itself, and I could understand why they anticipated it — the city was alive in a way that was not its ordinary life. Voices everywhere, music from different quarters at different hours, the specific smell of the roasting lamb that came on the Passover eve and stayed in the air for two days.

The cross selection happened on the third day of the feast.

Two soldiers came to the yard in the morning and walked the row of crosses with the same assessing gaze that the carpenter had brought to my hillside weeks before. They measured with their eyes and their hands — pressing the wood at the joint, checking the grain, standing back to assess the uprights. They moved along the row from the oldest to the newest and back again, conferring.

They stopped at me.

One of them pressed his palm flat against my upright and held it there. I had noticed this over the weeks — the way certain people's hands, when they rested against me, stayed longer than assessment required. He was not measuring anything when he did this. He was just present to the wood.

He said something to his companion. The other man looked at the joint where crossbeam met upright, checked the surfaces, nodded.

They marked me with chalk.

I would know this man again. Not because I could see his face, but because I know the quality of a hand that touches wood and stays. He would be the one still standing at the base of the hill when everyone else had gone, and he would be the one who said the thing that was true.

## The Move

They came back the next morning with ropes and a cart. The loading was straightforward — ropes around the middle of the upright, men lifting the crossbeam end while others guided the base. The lateral press of the ropes was a new sensation: compression from outside rather than the internal pressure of wind and weather that I had known for over a hundred years. I felt my grain take the load and distribute it, the joint holding.

The cart moved through the city.

The streets were narrow and the cross was long and progress was stop-and-start, the men calling ahead and then waiting while traffic cleared. I was horizontal on the cart, which gave me a changed view of the city: walls and gates and the underside of overhanging upper stories, the feet of people pressed to the sides of the street to let the cart through, faces looking down from windows.

The arsenal was near the Antonia Fortress on the north side of the Temple Mount. The soldiers there received the cross without ceremony and carried it inside.

The doors closed.

Stone walls on four sides and a stone ceiling and the smell of iron and old leather and oil and the particular cold of a place that does not see direct sun. Other equipment was stored there — armor, weapons, the organized tools of occupation. The cross was leaned against a wall in an area near the door with two others that were already there.

This was the first time I had been indoors.

The sound was different again: smaller than the yard, more contained. The city noise came through the stone walls reduced and flattened, losing its texture. I could hear Jerusalem out there — the Passover crowds, the Temple trumpets at the hours of prayer, the particular din of a city in festival — but from inside the stone walls it all arrived as a single low pressure, the way sound arrives from very far away.

Four days in here. That was how long it would be.

I did not know that then. I knew only the weight of the walls around me and the smell of iron and the muffled sound of the city where he was, somewhere, teaching and walking and pressing his step into the stone of the streets.

His step. That quality I had learned on my hillside, that I would have known anywhere.

He was out there in the city.

And I was in here.

And something in my grain — something I cannot explain in the language of wood — knew that the distance between us would close.
