# Via Dolorosa - Final Ascent

## The Gate

Good Friday, Mid-Morning - Northern Wall

The city gate was wide enough that the crowd thinned as we passed through it — the soldiers pushing people back on both sides, a brief moment of open air before the road narrowed again on the far side of the wall. I felt the change: inside the walls the sounds had bounced off stone and come back multiplied, a constant surrounding pressure of noise. Outside the wall the noise spread into open air and thinned, the crowd still there but the acoustics different, less enclosed.

The road beyond the gate rose more steeply. Golgotha was perhaps two hundred yards ahead — close enough now that I could see the rocky outcropping above the road, the pale limestone face of it catching the midmorning sun. A skull-shaped formation, if you had the angle for it. The Romans used it for what we were going to it for, which is why they had a name for it.

Simon was still at my lower end. His breathing had settled into a rhythm — the rhythm of a man doing hard physical work over a sustained distance, deliberate and measured. His grip had stopped adjusting. He had found the position and stayed in it.

The road steepened.

I felt it in both sets of hands. His, at my upper length — the grip tightening slightly as the angle of carry changed, more weight shifting forward. Simon's, at my lower end — the compensation going the other direction, bracing back against the downhill pull of my mass. Two men learning a shared physics in real time, their bodies solving it without conversation.

Then he went down again.

## The Fall

Both knees this time, and one hand. The impact came through my grain as a lurch and then stillness — the sudden deadweight of a man who has reached the floor of what his body will give him and is now negotiating with it. My weight shifted entirely to Simon, who caught it, staggered one step, held.

The soldiers stopped. One of them said something.

He did not move for a moment. Not the controlled pause of a man gathering himself — the longer pause of a man who is not sure yet what gathering himself will cost. His breathing against my wood was faster now, shallower. The hand on the ground was pressed flat against the road stones, fingers spread.

Then he pushed up.

It was not quick. It was the movement of someone spending something they are not certain they have enough of, the slow controlled rise of pure will working through a body that has run out of other resources. Halfway up he paused again — still on one knee, head down, the crown of thorns visible from above, the dried blood on his neck and shoulders dark in the full sun.

He got up.

Simon shifted the weight back. They found their balance. The procession moved.

I do not know how to tell you what it felt like to be the thing between them during that — to feel the fall through my grain and then feel the rising, the slow return of his weight to my upper length as he came back vertical. I was not afraid during it. I was not anything I have a word for. I was present to it entirely, the way a tree is present to weather: no distance, no interpretation, just the full reality of what was happening transmitted through wood.

## The Arrival

The last hundred yards were the steepest.

The road curved up to the right and the outcropping rose above us on the left, and then we were at the base of it and the soldiers were directing the procession off the road onto the bare limestone ground of the hill itself. The rock was uneven here — not the worn smoothness of the road but raw stone, the kind that has never had enough soil over it to grow anything more than sparse dry scrub at the edges.

Simon was released at the base. A soldier gestured and spoke and Simon stepped back, letting go of my lower end. The weight returned entirely to the man at my upper length. He took it without visible reaction — a readjustment, a steadying, and then still.

Simon stood to the side. I do not know what his face showed. I felt his hands leave my wood, which after the length of the road felt like a subtraction. He had been a stranger's hands an hour ago. An hour is enough time to know the quality of a grip.

The ground here was level enough for what came next. The soldiers knew the site — their movements had the efficiency of a task performed before, each man going to a position without being directed. Ropes, tools, the iron spikes in a leather bag that one of them carried. The ground had been prepared: there was a socket in the rock, cut square, deep enough for the base of a vertical beam to stand in.

He was still on his feet. Still holding my weight on his shoulders. Still breathing.

The sun was directly overhead now, or nearly — past the third hour since we left the fortress, approaching the sixth. The sky above Golgotha was pale and clear and very wide.

Around the hill, on three sides, the crowd had followed from the city and was settling into witness. Not a festival crowd anymore. The particular stillness of people who have come to see something they cannot look away from.

He stood.

I held.

The soldiers moved into position.
