# Chapter 8: Forces Converging

## The Roman Presence

*Late 32 CE - Early 33 CE*

Romans had always used my hillside.

Not this specific tree — though soldiers had rested in my shade on patrol, and a centurion once ate his midday meal sitting against my trunk with the ease of a man who knew how to be comfortable in unfamiliar terrain. But the hillside itself, the road below it, the angle of sight lines from my elevation toward Jerusalem's northwest wall — these were useful to people who thought in terms of terrain and control.

In the autumn of the thirty-second year they began coming with a different purpose.

A group arrived mid-morning: four soldiers and two men in civilian clothes whose bearing was still entirely military, carrying tablets and measuring instruments. They walked the perimeter of my hillside in a systematic pattern, noting positions, testing sight lines. One of them stood at my base and looked southeast toward the Temple Mount for a long time without moving.

Then they sat under me and the conversation was the flat-voiced coordinating kind I had learned to recognize — the sound of logistics. Not the questioning or emotional voices of the followers, not the ideological urgency of the religious delegations. Pure arrangement. Schedule. Specification.

They left after an hour.

More came. Not always soldiers — sometimes men who dressed as merchants or scholars but whose feet found the high ground instinctively, whose eyes measured distances. They would stop at the road's edge and look up at my hillside, then look toward the city, then back, and then note something and leave.

I am a tree. I do not know what they were calculating. But I had been on this hillside long enough to understand that these were people assessing where things would happen. The same way a shepherd reads a field before moving a flock — not interested in beauty, interested in function.

## Convergence

By winter the two streams were meeting.

The religious authorities had been coming for months now, their coordinated voices and purposeful movements giving them away. The Roman assessors had been coming for months. And then in the cold wet weeks before Passover they began arriving together — robed men and armored men sitting on the children's flat stones under my branches and talking with the specific evenness of people who have found an agreement and are now making it practical.

Their voices did not waver. There was no argument in the cadence. Something had been decided between them and what remained was arrangement.

The followers were still coming too, sometimes on the same days as these meetings. I would feel the two groups on my hillside simultaneously — one cluster under my upper branches speaking quietly in their searching urgent way, the other down the slope in the lower shade, their voices level and methodical. They did not appear to notice each other, though I felt the charge between them the way I feel electricity before lightning.

He passed on the road below less frequently now.

I had grown accustomed to his footstep as a regular calibration point — the specific press and release of weight that I could identify among all the other feet that used the road. When it appeared, the birds in my canopy settled. When it was absent, there was nothing exactly wrong, but a quality of the ordinary that was ordinary in the way a quiet house is ordinary after music has been playing.

In the weeks before Passover the road below my hill was dense with traffic — pilgrims arriving from every direction, the specific mixture of joy and exhaustion that Passover travel carried. Cart wheels and sandals and animals and the raised voices of families navigating crowds. Jerusalem filling beyond what its walls were built to hold.

And underneath all of it, the two groups still meeting on my hillside.

Something was close. I could feel it in the quality of the arrangements being made below me — the increasing specificity of the flat coordinating voices, the way their conversations had shortened from the longer discussions of autumn to the brief confirmations of people whose plan is complete.

My wood had finished its transformation. The heartwood was dense now in a way that had taken years. The grain was tight and dry and deeply saturated with the oil I had been pressing outward since the martyrs' blood came into my roots. I was as strong as I was going to get.

I did not know what was coming.
