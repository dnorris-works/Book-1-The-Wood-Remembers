# Chapter 7: Gathering Storm

## The New Visitors

*30-32 CE*

Something had changed in the people who came to my hillside.

It was not a sudden change. It came the way seasons come — gradually, the way you realize one morning that the light is different and has been different for some time without your having marked the exact day it shifted. But by the thirty-first year I was certain: the quality of presence on my hillside was not what it had been.

More people came. They came at different hours, not just travelers resting in midday heat but people arriving before dawn and staying through the dark. They came in small groups that moved carefully and spoke in the lowered voices of people being watched. Sometimes they sang, quietly, in the register that carries more inward than outward.

And they came after him.

I had seen him pass on the road below three times before he came to the hillside itself. I knew his footstep before I knew anything else about him — there was a quality to the weight he placed on the ground, a particular evenness. Animals in my branches went still when he was near. The birds that nested in my upper canopy and bolted at any unfamiliar sound would settle rather than scatter when his voice reached them.

The people who came after him told each other what they had heard and seen, in words I could not always follow, and their faces had the quality of people who have come back from somewhere they could not fully explain. They would stay under me for hours talking, and then go quiet, and then talk again, and the quality of their silence was different from ordinary silence — heavier, attended.

The other kind of visitor also began appearing.

These were different in their bearing and in the way they moved across my hillside. They came in delegations rather than groups — a composed, official purposefulness about their arrival that I had learned over decades to associate with authority. They sat under me on the flat stones and their conversation had the cadence of things being coordinated: the back and forth of position and counter-position, the particular rhythm of a plan being assembled.

I could not hear their words clearly but I could hear the quality of them.

The pilgrims and followers spoke with voices that climbed — wonder, argument, something like joy. The official visitors spoke with voices that went flat and horizontal, the sound of people who have decided and are now arranging. Where the followers lingered, these men moved efficiently and left.

## What the Air Carried

By winter of the thirty-second year the whole region felt different. I had lived through conflicts before — I had been on this hillside during the years of Roman consolidation and during the tax revolts and through various upheavals in the city — and I knew the quality of air when something large is gathering.

The smell of tension is real. I am a tree and I notice what enters through my bark and roots and leaves, and the chemistry of large numbers of frightened or angry people is something I can detect the way I detect coming rain. The air from Jerusalem carried it. Travelers arriving from the city brought it with them in their sweat and the specific sharpness of bodies held under stress.

Something was building.

The followers who came to my hillside were aware of it. Their conversations had changed in tone — the joy was still there but overlaid now with something that moved the other direction. They asked each other questions in voices that searched for reassurance. Some wept quietly. Others arrived alone and sat with their backs against my trunk in the posture of people who need something solid behind them.

He passed on the road below more frequently now. I could always identify his step.

The official delegations came with greater frequency. Sometimes they overlapped with the followers and I felt the specific charge in the air of these two kinds of people in proximity — the way the followers would go still and watchful, the way the delegates ignored them with the deliberate intention of a choice rather than genuine inattention.

The winter rains came and the hillside emptied somewhat, as it always did in the wet months, and I stood in the cold air and took in the rain through my bark and felt the season in my rings and attended to what was coming.

Spring would bring the Passover. The city filled beyond its walls during Passover and everyone on the roads felt the pressure of that convergence. But this spring would be different from the ones before it. I could feel the approach of it the way I had felt the approach of the dogs on the night I held the girl — something directional, something that knew exactly where it was going.

I had been standing on this hillside for more than a hundred years. My roots went deep into the limestone. The martyrs' blood had hardened my heartwood. The scar where I had held the girl was part of my grain.

I had been waiting, though I could not have said for what.

The storm was nearly here.
