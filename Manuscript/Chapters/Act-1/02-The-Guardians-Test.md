# Chapter 2: The Guardian's Test

## The Children

They came first when I was perhaps ten years old — three children from a village below the hill, drawn up the slope by nothing more than the curiosity of children and the particular quality of my hillside that had begun to draw people's eyes.

The girl was the one who saw me first. Her name was Miriam. She walked all the way around my trunk with her hands extended, just barely not touching, the way children test a thing they want to make contact with but haven't decided about yet. Then she put both palms flat against my bark and stood still for a long moment.

"It knows we're here," she said.

The boy named Benjamin laughed. He was the loud one, the one who jumped from rocks and proposed games and always ran ahead on the path. "Trees don't know anything."

"This one does," Miriam said, and did not move her hands.

The third child, Aaron, said nothing. He was the quiet one. He stood back from the other two and looked up into my canopy with his serious young face and I felt, from him, something I had not felt from a human being before: genuine curiosity about me specifically, not about the hillside or the view or the shade I could offer, but about what I was.

They came back. Week after week through that first season and then through the seasons that followed. They brought other children sometimes and then came alone again. Miriam began arranging stones at my base — smooth ones she found on the road, round ones she carried up from the valley. A circle of them around my roots. Over months the circle grew more deliberate, more carefully placed, until it was clearly something she had made rather than found.

Benjamin brought wildflowers woven into my lower branches. Aaron brought nothing at first, then one afternoon he brought a small written thing — I could not read it, did not need to — and tucked it into a crack in my bark with the seriousness of someone completing a task they had thought about for a long time.

They came through the years and they grew, and the circle of stones and the braided grass and the careful attention accumulated into something that the other travelers and pilgrims who rested on the hillside began to recognize and respect without knowing quite what it was.

I held them as I held everything. In the grain, in the rings, in the slow chemistry of what a tree remembers.

They were the first to see me. Not a tree on a hillside. Me.

## The Night

The afternoon Miriam came with white flowers she had found near a spring, the boys wanted to leave before the heat got worse. She told them to go ahead, she would finish and follow. Benjamin and Aaron went down the path calling back to her, and then their voices were gone and it was just the two of us and the sound of her fingers in the flowers.

I felt the shift in the ground before I heard anything.

Multiple feet, weight distributed low, the specific press of animals moving with intention rather than wandering. Not the deer that sometimes came to the hill or the goats from the village below — this was different. The vibration came from the deeper hills to the north where the ground stayed rocky and dry all summer.

Then I heard them.

The girl looked up from her work at the sound of the first growl. I felt the change in her immediately — the way a person's weight shifts when fear arrives. Her hand found my bark. I felt her palm flat against me, fingers spread, the kind of grip that looks for something to hold.

The dogs had spread across the slope. I could see them from my height: five animals, lean and pale, circling to close the paths below.

I moved my branch.

I want to say more about it than that, but I cannot. It was a choice and then it was done — the branch dropping toward her, the angle changing in ways that wood does not naturally change. She understood immediately. Her hands found it and she pulled herself up and I held her weight.

The dogs hit my trunk. Their claws scraped bark. They leaped and fell back and leaped again and I held still against the impact of each one. Her feet were above their reach. I kept them there.

The night came.

She was cold. I shifted my leaves against the wind where I could. When she grew tired and her grip slipped, I felt it before it happened and moved slightly, just enough. I was not able to do this without cost — the branch held at an angle my wood was not made for, and I felt the stress of it in every fiber along the joint where branch met trunk. Not pain in the way a person knows pain, but something. A burning in the wood where the grain pulled against itself.

I held it.

The dogs settled at my base and watched upward through the dark.

Sometime before dawn she fell into an exhausted sleep and I held her weight through her stillness, which was heavier in its way than her movement had been. Stars crossed overhead. Jerusalem's lights were visible in the southeast, faint and constant. An owl moved through the dark to the north. The dogs lay below and waited.

The men who came looking for her arrived with the first light, calling her name across the hillside. The dogs scattered at the sound of many feet. She woke at her father's voice and called down to him, and then she was climbing off my branch and I felt her weight leave.

I tried to return my branch to its natural position.

It would not go all the way back. The long night had set the wood at the new angle. Where I had held her, the grain had shifted and locked, and now the curve was part of me — visible to anyone who looked: a branch bent toward the road at a height a child's hands could reach.

She knew what had happened. Before she left with her family she turned and put her hand against my bark again, and she spoke words I could not parse but whose quality I understood. Then she was gone down the hill with the others.

## After

The branch stayed curved.

Travelers noticed it. They would stop and look and discuss it in voices that carried recognition of something, though they didn't always agree on what. Some brought other people to see. The curve became a marker: the tree where the child had been saved, northwest of the road, on the hillside. People began leaving things at my base — stones arranged in patterns, braided grass, small offerings of bread. Some touched the branch itself with careful hands.

The children came back. They were quieter now, or perhaps different in the quality of their quiet — less the noise of games, more the attention of young people who have encountered something real. They sat under me and talked with the seriousness of people who know that the world can be more than ordinary.

I grew.

My roots found the limestone cracks and went deeper, threading through rock toward the water table. My trunk thickened. The canopy spread until I cast a circle of shade twenty feet across at midday. Birds nested in my upper branches each spring, returning to the same forks, confident in some way that animals express through simple return.

Animals rested at my base — goats, sometimes, and once a deer that stayed through the heat of an afternoon and left when the shadows lengthened. They settled with the ease of creatures that know where it is safe to be still.

What I understood from the branch was this: I had been made for something. The years of growth, the roots reaching deeper each season, the slow accumulation of rings — I had been building toward a capacity I had not chosen but had been given. The night with the girl had not been the purpose. It had been the demonstration that I could hold when it mattered.

Something larger was coming.

I did not know its shape. The travelers who paused in my shade spoke with increasing urgency about things I could not follow — names, prophecies, a kingdom, a teacher — voices dropping low and then rising again with the heat of argument. The air across the whole region carried a quality like the atmosphere before a storm: everything sharpened, everything waiting.

I grew toward the light and sank my roots and waited for whatever I was being prepared to hold.

The branch stayed curved.

It stays curved still.
