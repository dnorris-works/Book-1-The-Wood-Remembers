# Chapter 20: The Raising

## The Raising

Good Friday, Late Morning - Golgotha

The ropes went taut.

I felt them first — hemp fibers biting into the wood where they'd been knotted, the grain compressing under the pull. My weight shifted. I had been flat against the stone since they'd laid me down to receive him, my wood warm against the rock, and now the pulling began and the rock scraped along my back as they dragged the base toward the hole they'd cut.

The tilt started slowly. Horizontal to something like thirty degrees. I'd spent my whole life vertical, roots down, crown up, the whole weight of myself hanging from the sky. Being horizontal had felt wrong since the moment they felled me. Now the tilting back toward upright felt like returning to something I'd almost forgotten.

Fifty degrees. Sixty.

The ropes pulled from above. Poles shoved from below, the soldiers' boots scraping on the limestone for purchase. I could hear the effort in them — grunts, shouted coordination in Latin I didn't need to translate. Seventy degrees.

Then the base dropped.

The impact when the foot of the cross hit the bottom of the cut hole traveled through every ring of my grain from the base to the top of the vertical beam. A jolt that would have shaken leaves off me if I'd still had leaves. The crossbeam shuddered. The joint where crossbeam met upright, mortise and tenon held by iron, groaned under the sudden redistribution of weight.

I was vertical.

For a moment the ropes were the only thing keeping me from toppling forward. Then they wedged stone against my base, packing the hole, and the ropes went slack, and I held myself upright on my own for the first time since Golgotha.

The wind hit differently up here. Stronger. I hadn't expected that. On the hillside where I'd grown, I'd known that wind for a hundred years. This was the same wind but higher and without anything between me and it — no grove, no valley curve, nothing. Just the stone plateau and the open air and the crowd below.

The crowd was larger than I'd realized when I was lying down.

Thousands of them, packed to the edges of the plateau and spreading down the slope. Their noise came up to me in waves. Not uniform noise — different voices doing different things, arguments and weeping and something that might have been prayer, all of it rising and mixing in the hot morning air.

I could see Jerusalem from here. All of it. The Temple catching the sun on its western face, the walls, the rooftops packed with people who'd come out to watch from a distance. The hills beyond.

I had grown for a hundred twenty years looking at that city. This was the first time I'd been tall enough to see over its walls.

## The Weight

I felt him before I saw him clearly.

A vibration in the stone under my base, rhythmic — many feet, a procession moving toward the plateau. The crowd's noise changed. The arguments didn't stop but something shifted in them, a different quality, the way a room changes when someone walks in who changes everything.

The soldiers brought him up the slope.

He was close to finished. I could tell even from the distance, watching the way he moved — or rather watching the way the soldiers held him up to make him move. The flogging had left marks I could see across the plateau. The crown of thorns had been pressed down so that blood ran in lines down his face and had dried there.

He looked at the cross.

I don't know what he saw. I only know that he stopped walking for a moment, not because the soldiers stopped — they didn't — but as if he stopped inside himself first, and his body followed by half a breath.

Then he came forward.

They brought him to the base of the cross and turned him. The moment his back touched my wood I felt him — really felt him, the way I'd felt rain and wind and the hands of the men who'd worked me. His shoulder blades against my vertical beam. His arms reaching back along my crossbeam as they stretched them out.

He was breathing hard. Each exhale pressed his back into my wood.

His weight against me. His warmth. One hundred twenty years of growing toward something I hadn't understood, and now I was holding it — holding him — and I still didn't understand it, but I could feel it the way wood feels the grain of another piece pressed against it. Something in me recognized something in him.

The soldiers moved to his wrists.

## The Nailing

The hammer rose.

I waited.

The first nail entered my crossbeam with a sound like the world cracking open.

Not metaphor. Actual sound — my grain splitting, fibers tearing, wood cells rupturing as iron forced passage through heartwood that had grown in darkness over a hundred sixteen years. The splitting traveled up my length in waves I felt as shockwaves, each ring of growth protesting the violation.

But not breaking. Making room.

My wood compressed around the iron, fibers bending rather than shattering completely. The nail was thick — Roman efficiency demanded thickness for weight-bearing. My grain wrapped around it like fingers closing on something that would never be released.

The second nail went through his wrist.

I felt that impact through the touching-point where his flesh pressed my wood. Felt the moment iron punched through bone and tendon, felt his body convulse, felt the strangled sound he made that wasn't quite a scream but exceeded any category of suffering I'd witnessed in a hundred sixteen years of watching Jerusalem's violence.

The nail exited his wrist and entered my wood six inches from the first.

My grain split along the fault line, the crack racing down my length. I should have broken. The stress exceeded what olive wood could bear. But I didn't break. Couldn't break. Wouldn't break.

The third nail. Fourth. Fifth and sixth securing the crossbeam to the vertical post.

Each invasion of iron making my wood ring soundlessly. Each impact sending vibration through my entire being.

But I was still here. Still holding.

Each nail was also in me.

## The First Hour

When they had finished nailing, the crowd settled into something different than it had been — less movement, more stillness, as if everyone had taken a breath and was deciding whether to let it out.

His weight hung from my crossbeam now through the nails in his wrists. To breathe he had to push up against the nail in his feet — I felt each effort as a shift of load, his legs driving down to lift his chest, the sound of each breath bought at that price.

Slow. Labored. But present.

I held him.

Below, the voices had separated into groups. Some were shouting up at him — I couldn't hear individual words in the noise, only the mockery in the tone. Some were further back, watching in silence. Very close to my base, almost close enough to touch my wood, a small group of women stood together. They had not moved since he was raised. They were not going to move.

The sun was past its zenith. The light was sharp and the stone gave back heat from below while the sun pressed it from above. The blood from his crown had dried. More was moving from his wrists, following the grain of my wood in thin lines.

His blood smelled like copper and something else I had no name for. Something that had been in the soil of this hillside for as long as I could remember, older than the city below, older than the road.

I held the smell in my grain the way wood holds rain.

The hours moved.
