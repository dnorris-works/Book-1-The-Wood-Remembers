# The Last Words

## I Thirst

The light had been back for a while when he spoke again.

Not long — the shadows were still angled low from the west, the stone still cool from the three hours of dark. But the soldiers had resumed their positions, the crowd had resettled into its loose arrangement on the hillside, and the ordinary sounds of the afternoon had returned. A bird somewhere. The wind moving through the dry grass at the hill's edge.

His voice was barely a voice by then. More breath than sound. I felt the familiar effort first — the push against the nail, the shift of weight on the crossbeam — and then two words, and the sound of them was so dry, so stripped of everything that voice usually carries, that it took me a moment to understand they were words at all.

The soldiers nearest the base moved. One went quickly to where the supplies were kept, came back with something on the end of a long stalk — hyssop, from the smell of it, dipped in the sour wine they carried. He lifted it toward the crossbeam. I felt the slight shift of weight as contact was made.

The women watched. The young man behind them watched. No one spoke.

The wind moved again, and this time it carried nothing — no rain smell, no promise. Just air off the western hills, dry and ordinary.

His breathing after that was the quietest it had been. Not labored the way it had been in the middle hours, when the effort was visible and countable. This was something else. Shallow. Widely spaced. The kind of breathing that is not yet finished but is close to finished, and knows it.

I held him in my wood and I held still.

## It Is Finished

He gathered himself one more time.

I felt it before it happened — a change in the way his weight rested on the crossbeam, a tension moving through him that was different from the mechanical effort of breathing. Something deliberate. Something chosen.

The push against the nail. The lift of his chest. And then his voice, and this time it had volume — not the hoarse whisper of the two words before, not the cry from the darkness, but a full voice, the voice of a man who has decided to speak and means to be heard.

Three words. Or one word — I could not tell, in the language he used, whether it was one thing or three. It fell on the hill like a stone falls on still water, the sound of it spreading outward past the crowd, past the road below, out toward the walls of the city where it could no longer be heard but had already gone.

The crowd did not react the way they had reacted to the cry in the darkness. No confusion, no calling back and forth. They went still. Some of the people who had been standing at a distance took a step closer, as if they weren't sure they had heard correctly and needed to confirm it.

It was finished. Whatever it was. Whatever word that was.

The women below me did not move. The one with her hand against my bark — I felt her press harder for a moment, a single involuntary tightening of the fingers against the wood, and then she released, and her hand rested flat and still against my grain.

The soldiers said nothing. The bird that had been audible somewhere behind the hill had stopped.

Everything was very quiet.

## Father

His breathing after the declaration was different from everything that had come before.

In the morning, the rhythm had been effortful but steady — countable, mechanical, a body doing what bodies do to stay alive. Through the dark hours it had slowed and deepened into something more irregular, the pauses longer, the returns uncertain. After the declaration it was almost nothing. A faint movement of weight on the crossbeam. A stillness. A faint movement again.

I had no way to count how many breaths passed between that and the last one.

When it came, I felt it as a gathering again — the same gathering I had felt before each time he spoke, the deliberate marshaling of what was left. His weight shifted on the crossbeam as his chest rose. The muscles I had felt working through the wood all day made one more effort.

He spoke toward the sky. Not toward the crowd, not toward the women below, not toward the men dying on either side of him. Toward the sky, or past the sky, toward whatever he understood to be above it.

One sentence. I felt the breath that carried it leave his body and not return.

The weight on my crossbeam did not change immediately. The body remained as it was — suspended, present, the nails holding what they held. But the quality of the weight was different. The subtle constant motion that had been present all day — the micro-shifts of a living body making continual small adjustments, the evidence of breath and heartbeat distributed through the wood — was gone.

He was still. Completely still.

I had held living things before. I had held the weight of nesting birds in my branches, and the press of a man's hand against my bark, and the cumulative living weight of a hundred seasons of growth. I knew the difference between the stillness of a thing resting and the stillness of a thing finished.

Below me, one of the women made a sound.

The young man beside her put his hand on her shoulder. He did not speak. There was nothing to say that would help and he seemed to know it.

The soldiers looked up. The one who had been in charge of the detail walked toward the base of the crosses and looked for a long time at what I was holding.

I recognized the quality of his looking. It was the same quality as his hands in the arsenal — not the attention of a soldier doing his work but the attention of a man who has been brought up against something he cannot account for.

He said something. His voice was low and I could not hear the whole of it above the wind. What reached me was three words. I have held them in the grain the same way I have held the nails, the blood, the warmth that did not cool. Three words from a man who had touched my wood in a storage room and then stood at my base and understood something I could not have told him.

He turned and looked at his men. None of them said anything.

I held him in my wood.

The sun continued west. The shadows on the hill stretched long.
