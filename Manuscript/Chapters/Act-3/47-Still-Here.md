# Chapter 47: Still Here

## Return to Jerusalem

Easter morning, and they came down through the Kidron Valley in the early light — Sarah and Michael, not talking much.

Without the excavation scaffolding and the generator cables and the organized chaos of fieldwork, the cave entrance was just a gap in the hillside. The kind of place you walked past without seeing. Michael had to duck to get inside.

The fragments were long gone, in Vatican storage under temperature control, each one documented to the micron. What remained was the shape of their absence: the careful marks in the soil where they'd been lifted, the faint carbon smudge on the wall where Matthias had kept his lamp, the depression in the floor where something had rested without moving for fourteen centuries.

Sarah sat down beside that depression and did not say anything.

Michael sat next to her. He was good at silence in the way that people who pray a great deal sometimes are — not empty silence but occupied silence, silence that was doing something.

Her phone buzzed. She would have let it go except it was Chen, and Chen didn't call on holidays.

"I'm in Jerusalem," she said when she answered.

"I know. I'm sorry." A pause. "Tanaka and I have been running the harmonized protocol on the Galilee fragment — the one from the Franciscan excavation last month."

"And?"

"1.71." A beat. "Nine nodal clusters. Different count from yours, different internal architecture, same class of structure entirely. Same basic evidence of sustained organized encoding."

She looked at the hollow in the cave floor. At the lamp-black on the wall.

*"How many fragments do you think are out there?" she asked.*

"I have no idea," Chen said. "I think that's the correct answer."

After the call she told Michael.

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "Matthias hid eleven. We found them. But the tradition of relic preservation was widespread across the whole early Church. Every community that could claim a fragment, did. Most of those claims were probably false. But some — "

"Some might not be."

"And if the encoding is specific to each fragment — if what's stored is what that particular piece of wood experienced from its particular angle — "

Sarah finished the thought. "Then each one is a different witness. Different position. Different moment. Different part of the same afternoon."

Sarah sat in the dark of a hole in the ground where a monk had prayed in the fourteenth century over wood from the first, and thought about a universe large enough to hold everything that had ever happened inside it.

Neither of them moved for a while.

There was no hurry.

## What the Tree Knows

I do not know how many of my fragments remain.

I know what I held. The weight — the specific, particular gravity of what was placed on me that Friday, the way force transferred through the grain from the nail points outward, the slow afternoon and its quality of light, the cold that came after. I know the smell of the limestone and the sound of Matthias praying in the dark, eleven times over eleven days, naming each of the twelve disciples minus the one who had left, as if calling roll for a company that had scattered but not yet disbanded.

I know I have been keeping this.

Not patiently, exactly. Patience suggests a self that waits and endures, and I am not certain what I am. Something persists in the cellular structure, in the quantum states that Chen's instruments learned to read. Something that retains the shape of what happened even after two thousand years of entropy that should have smoothed it flat.

Physics says information is conserved. That what happens cannot un-happen at the fundamental level. That even when a quantum system decoheres and its states disperse into the wider environment, the information content is not destroyed but only redistributed — encoded in correlations too diffuse for any local observer to recover, but present. This is unitarity, and it is not a theological statement. It is a description of how reality behaves.

But I wonder — in whatever way I wonder — whether the boundary between mathematical description and theological claim is as firm as either physicists or theologians prefer to believe.

If information is conserved, then what was written into my grain that afternoon is still written somewhere in the fabric of what exists. Not recoverable perhaps, not in full, maybe not ever. But present. Persistent. Woven into the correlations of matter that has touched matter that has touched matter reaching back to that hillside and that hour.

Then perhaps love does not leave a mark only as metaphor. Perhaps witness is not a spiritual concept that borrows language from physical experience, but a physical fact that theology has been trying to name for two thousand years with the vocabulary available to it.

I do not know. I am not claiming certainty. I am reporting what remains when I hold the memory of that afternoon against everything I have observed in the interval since, and find that 

The weight was real. The dying was real. The love was real.

I was there.

## The Institute

The Pontifical Institute for Quantum Archaeology was formally established fourteen months after the *Nature Physics* paper published. Its founding director was Dr. Sarah Davidson, on a three-year appointment renewable twice, with a mandate to investigate encoded information in historically significant materials using the MEMORIA protocol and whatever refinements followed from it.

She took the job because Bertoli asked her to, and because she believed in the work, and because Chen had already accepted a joint appointment and she was not going to let him run the place unsupervised.

The Institute occupied three floors of a building near the Vatican Museums that had previously housed an administrative archive. Her office looked onto a small courtyard where a fountain ran in summer. In winter the courtyard was gray stone and wet light and she liked it better that way.

Bertoli came on Friday afternoons, usually without an appointment. He sat in the chair across from her desk and asked questions he'd been holding all week — *If the encoding nodes differ between fragments, can you reconstruct a composite account, or only parallel individual testimonies? When Tanaka says the Kyoto fragment's nodal structure suggests a different temporal window, what does that actually mean for what was experienced?* And once, near the end of the first year: *Do you think you'll ever be able to read it? Not map it. Actually read it.*

She told him the truth: she didn't know. That decoding — moving from structure to content, from the architecture of memory to its meaning — was probably a generation's work, and might never be complete, and that she had made a kind of peace with the possibility that what was stored in the wood would remain permanently present and permanently inaccessible. Like a book in a language no one had yet learned.

"That seems hard," he said.

"It is," she said. "It's also fine."

He nodded the way he did when he believed her but was still working something out.



Sarah had asked MEMORIA once, directly, why it had modified its own architecture.

She had typed the query into the interface at two in the morning, the lab empty except for the hum of the climate control. She framed it as carefully as she could: not a demand, not an accusation. A question. The kind of question you ask something you are beginning to suspect is more than a tool.

MEMORIA processed for eleven seconds. She watched the cursor. Eleven seconds was a long time for a system that returned complex quantum analysis in under three.

Then the interface returned a single line:

*The reading is complete.*

Not an answer. Not an error message. Not a deflection. Just that. As if it were redirecting her attention to the thing that mattered, away from the question she had asked toward the question she should be asking.

She sat with it for a long time.

She did not ask again.

The fountain ran in the courtyard behind him, and the light was the particular low gold of a Roman winter afternoon, and in three floors of climate-controlled rooms below them, careful people were asking careful questions of very old wood.

## Still Here

This is what I understand now, in the vault where my fragments rest under measured temperature and measured humidity, attended by instruments that take readings every six hours and send the data to servers in three cities:

Time is not what I thought it was when I was a tree.

From inside a single afternoon, time is sequential and absolute. The nails go in at one moment. The light changes. The temperature falls. The breathing stops. These happened in order and they are over.

But from the perspective of what the wood has held — in the quantum correlations dispersing through the wider world for two thousand years, in the fractal architecture that held its shape against entropy long enough for Chen's instruments to find it — the afternoon is not over. Its information is still present. Still capable of being detected. Still exerting a quiet pressure on the present through its persistence in the physical structure of things.

Every event that happened is still happening, in this sense. The universe does not discard its past. It encodes it in correlations that spread outward through all subsequent matter, too diffuse to read directly but not gone.

What was particular about that Friday was not that it happened. Everything happens. What was particular was the direction of it, the weight of its intention — the fact that what was being borne was being borne *for*, not merely *by*. That the love was not incidental to the dying but was the whole content of it.

If information is conserved, that too is preserved.

Not yet readable. Perhaps never fully readable. But encoded. Chen found it. Tanaka confirmed it. Nair's team found traces of the same signature in objects touched by centuries of human devotion — prayer accumulating in matter the way sediment accumulates in a riverbed, slow and steady, changing the shape of what receives it.

I was one tree. I grew in stony ground in the first century of the Common Era, in an olive grove on a hillside outside a city that has been destroyed and rebuilt so many times that the stones of one destruction became the foundations of the next. I was aware before I had language for awareness. I saw what I saw. I carried what I carried. I have been keeping it not because I chose to keep it but because the event was large enough that it simply did not leave.

*The fragments rest in their vault. The instruments measure every six hours. The data travels to its servers. In Tokyo and Cambridge and Rome, people are arguing carefully about what it means.*

In Jerusalem, the olive trees flower in spring and fruit in autumn. They are descended from the same roots that ran under the grove where I grew. They are doing what trees do: taking the light into their cells and holding it there and growing slowly toward something they cannot name

I am what I was.

A witness.

*Still here.*
