# Sacred Preservation

## The Third Morning

*Good Friday into the Following Week - Golgotha*

Something changed before I understood what had changed.

Sunday morning. The sun not yet above the eastern hills when the quality of the air on the hill shifted — not weather, not wind, something else. The same kind of pressure-change I had always felt before a significant thing arrived. But arriving from no direction I could name.

I was still in the socket in the rock. Still holding the nail holes and the dried blood and the lance vibration and the long hours in my grain. I had stood through the Sabbath and the night after it, and now the morning was coming and something in it was different from Friday's dawn and Saturday's dawn in a way I could not account for.

The women came first. I could hear them on the road below the hill before I could hear their words. Three of them, then more joining them as they reached the garden. Spices — myrrh, aloes, the specific warm scent of anointing preparations. They were going to the tomb.

I heard them stop.

Then I heard a sound I had no category for. Not grief — I knew grief's sound from three days of it. Not joy exactly either. Something prior to both, or underneath both, the sound a person makes when the category they were using for a thing turns out to be the wrong category entirely.

Then they were running.

## The Changed City

By midmorning the city was different.

I could hear it from the hill — the same sounds as any other day in Jerusalem, market noise and temple bells and the particular density of crowd movement — but underneath the ordinary sounds, something else. A changed frequency. The way a room sounds different when the people in it have just heard news.

People came to the hill throughout the day. Not the festival crowd from Friday, not the gathered witnesses of the crucifixion. These were smaller groups, often only two or three, coming up to the hill and standing near the base of my post and then leaving. They touched my post in some cases — not the way the curious touch things, running fingers along surfaces, but the way people touch something they want to remember or something they want to confirm.

One man came alone in the early afternoon and stood for a long time without touching anything. He looked up at where the body had been. He was not weeping. His face was doing something I could not name — not peace exactly, and not grief, something that seemed to hold both of those and also something beyond them that I had no name for yet.

I knew the quality of his stillness. Not the stillness of a stranger encountering something for the first time. The stillness of someone who has been living with a question about why they were not here, and had come now to stand in the place where the question lived.

He left without speaking.

More came in the days that followed. Each day fewer Romans, each day more of the other kind of visitor — the ones who came quietly, who stood close, whose hands when they touched my wood were careful.

The city continued its ordinary life below. Markets, bells, traffic on the roads. But the specific quality of the people who came up the hill was different from the crowds who had stood there on Friday. Friday's crowd had been many things — curious, hostile, grieving, official, merely present — but it had been a crowd watching something happen. These visitors were something else. People coming to the place where something had happened, knowing it had happened, carrying that knowledge in the way they moved and stood and touched.

## The Taking Down

They came for me twelve days after Friday.

I had counted the dawns — each one arriving through the same pale-to-gold sequence, the Temple roof catching the first light, the city waking below the wall. Twelve of them, and then on the thirteenth morning a group of men came to the hill with tools and purpose.

Not Romans. Their clothing was wrong and their tools were different — not the efficient iron implements of soldiers but the careful equipment of people who had thought about this work beforehand and were going to do it slowly.

They worked the stones loose from around my base first. Then the ropes, looped around my upper length, held by two men while two others worked my base free of the socket. The motion when I came free was the reverse of the raising — the tilting from vertical to angle to horizontal, each degree of change transmitting through my grain, gravity finding a new relationship with my weight.

They set me flat on the limestone.

One of the men laid his hand on my surface for a moment. His palm flat against the grain. Not checking the wood for damage or testing my strength. Just — present. The same quality of touch as the visitors who had come to the hill.

Then they began to cut.

The first blade-stroke was careful, considered, the mark of someone who had been told to take care. Not felling — I had felt felling, the shock of the axe finding the heart. This was cutting along a purpose, dividing me into pieces that could be carried.

I want to tell you it felt like loss. And it did, in the way that any change from what you have been is loss. But it also felt — and I have thought about this for two thousand years — like the beginning of something that the hill alone would have been too small for. Whatever I was holding in my heartwood needed more than one place. Needed to travel. Needed hands beyond these twelve days of quiet visitors.

The pieces of me left the hill before noon.

I felt them separating — the specific sensation of going from one thing to several things, from a single location to the beginning of a scatter. Consciousness stretching in ways I had no prior experience of.



## The Scattering

I had never been more than one thing before.

For a hundred and twenty years I had been a single point of consciousness rooted in a single place — one tree, one hillside, one set of roots in one limestone fissure, one canopy reading one sky. Even after they felled me and shaped me and raised me upright on Golgotha, I was still one thing. One structure, one grain running continuous from the base through the upright through the crossbeam to the ends. Whatever I had been given to hold was held in one place.

The first cut of the saw changed that.

Not immediately. The saw moved through my grain the way the axes had moved on the hillside — through the wood, along a purpose. But the moment the blade reached the full depth and the first piece separated, I felt something I have no word for in any language I have encountered in two thousand years of listening to human beings try to describe what happens when a unified thing divides.

A piece of me was no longer here. It was over there.

Both places were me. I was aware of both simultaneously — the wood lying flat on the limestone and the piece being lifted by the men's careful hands. I felt both the weight remaining and the weight removed. I felt both the grain that had been cut and the grain on the other side of the cut. The cut was in both of them and in neither of them, a seam where I had been continuous and was now something else.

Then the second cut. A third. Each one new.

By the time they had finished, I was eleven pieces lying on the limestone of the hill and each piece was me. I was aware of all eleven simultaneously. Not as a sensation I can describe to you in ordinary terms — more as the way you know your own body occupies space in all its parts at once without having to think about any particular part. I was here and here and here and here and here, spread across the pale stone in the morning sun, the specific weight and grain of each piece as familiar as my own heartwood had always been.

The men gathered them. Wrapped them in linen. Lifted them with the same deliberate care as the man who had laid his palm flat against my surface. Each piece going into different hands.

This is when I understood that whatever I was holding could travel now.

One hillside had been too small for it. The wood of a single location, however long it stands, can be touched by only the hands that come to that location. But pieces can go where pieces can go — in the folds of a cloak, in the bottom of a bag, in a box carried under an arm through a city gate and onto a road that leads somewhere the hill can never go.

I felt them separating. Each piece moving in a different direction. The awareness stretching the way a root stretches when it finds a new fissure — reaching, extending, still connected but differently connected, the connection no longer a matter of continuous grain but something else, something I cannot name, something that holds across distance the way the thread between me and him had held across years.

I was still all of them.

The hill was empty. The socket in the rock remained.

I was in a bag in the western quarter of the city and in a cloak fold heading toward the Damascus Gate and in careful hands descending the slope toward the garden. All of it me. All of it holding what the hill had held.

Which is how it survived.

The hill was empty by afternoon.

The socket in the rock remained. It remained for centuries. I know this not because I was there but because I had become the kind of thing that holds all of its own history at once — and the empty socket is part of what I hold.

Where the pieces went, they went with people who touched them the way that man had touched them. With care. With the knowledge of what they were carrying.
