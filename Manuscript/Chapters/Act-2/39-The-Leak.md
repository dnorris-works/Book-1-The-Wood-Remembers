# Chapter 39: The Leak

## Five Days Later

The email had a subject line designed to stop the heart: *Your MEMORIA Data in Tomorrow's Issue — Skeptical Inquiry.*

Sarah found it at 7 AM, before she'd finished her first cup of coffee. She read it standing at her kitchen counter, still in her coat from the walk in.

*Dr. Davidson,*

*We're publishing an exposé tomorrow on "Quantum Mysticism in Biblical Archaeology." Our source provided preliminary MEMORIA data from your Jerusalem project showing molecular patterns you've allegedly interpreted as "preserved consciousness." Our physics consultants have reviewed the raw data and find no evidence supporting your extraordinary claims. We're giving you 24 hours to comment before publication.*

*—Dr. Marcus Reinhardt, Editor-in-Chief*

She scrolled to the attachments. Her preliminary findings. Unpublished. Raw data, calibration parameters, the complete MEMORIA methodology — proprietary information that represented five years of doctoral and postdoctoral work. Information that should have existed in exactly two places: the Vatican's secure server and her encrypted backup drive.

She called Patterson from the kitchen, still in her coat.

"Who had access?" he asked, once she'd explained.

She ran through the list. Father Kowalski. Cardinal Bertoli. The Vatican's research team. And —

Webb had run the secondary calibration protocols every morning without being asked. He had caught an indexing error in the baseline dataset that would have invalidated three weeks of readings. He had stayed in the lab until eleven on a Tuesday to finish a data verification run because he wanted the numbers clean before she reviewed them in the morning. She had trusted him with the server access because he was the kind of person you trusted with server access.

Her stomach dropped. "Dr. Marcus Webb."

"Your postdoc? But he's been with you three years."

"He applied for the Stanford quantum archaeology position last month. Didn't get it." She felt sick. "He blamed me. Said my 'religious entanglements' were poisoning the field's reputation. He told me two weeks ago that 'someone needs to stop you before you turn quantum archaeology into Catholic apologetics.'"

Patterson was already making calls. Twenty minutes later, security confirmed it: Webb had accessed her lab server twelve times over three weeks, copying encrypted files to an external drive each time. The drive traced to an IP address registered to *Skeptical Inquiry*'s editorial offices.

Then a message arrived from Father Kowalski, encrypted: *Vatican security detected unusual access to our shared files three days ago. IP address traces to Harvard. We assumed it was you. Was it?*

It wasn't.

She sat down on the kitchen floor. Not in a chair — the floor, her back against the cabinet, her coat still on.

Three years. Webb had been in her lab for three years. She had written his fellowship recommendation. She had given him primary access to the MEMORIA server because it was easier than doing the transfers manually and because she trusted him the way you trust someone who shows up early and stays late and asks the right questions. She had trusted him the specific way you trust a person who seems to care about the same things you care about.

She sat with that for a minute. Then she stood up.

Sarah sat down at the table. The MEMORIA protocol details, the quantum signatures, the fragment locations — he'd handed all of it to people who would use her own data as ammunition against her. He'd chosen the journal carefully. He knew exactly what they'd do with it.

She thought about what kind of person does that. Not a cynical one — a true believer. Not in faith, but in materialism. Webb genuinely thought he was protecting science. He'd looked at her work and decided that destroying it was a form of care.

He didn't know what he'd done. That wasn't an excuse. It was just true.

"What do you want to do?" Patterson asked.

Sarah thought of Hermann Vogel, the 17th-century printer who'd protected fragments even as soldiers broke his fingers. Of her grandmother's seventy years of prayer. Of Father Kowalski's voice in the cave: *What if intellectual honesty means following evidence wherever it leads?*

"Respond to *Skeptical Inquiry* with the complete findings, properly peer-reviewed and published first. File a report for data theft and breach of confidentiality — I'll decide whether to pursue it after I've spoken with Webb directly. And let Harvard decide what it values."

She hung up and did not move for a while.

The kitchen was exactly as she’d left it that morning. The coffee she’d been making when the email arrived was cold. Outside, Cambridge was starting its ordinary day — someone’s car in the street, the clang of a recycling truck two blocks over, the world continuing without any reference to what had just happened in her kitchen.

She thought about what kind of person does what Webb had done. Not a cynical one — a true believer. Not in faith, but in materialism. He had looked at her work and decided that stopping it was a form of care. He genuinely thought he was protecting something.

That made it worse, not better. It meant there was nothing she could have done. No conversation, no data, no peer review process that would have reached him, because he had already decided what the data meant before it existed.

She sat on the kitchen floor, back against the cabinet, and stayed there long enough for the recycling truck to finish the block and move on.
