# Second Light (Discovery)

## The Initial Find

*January 12, 2025 - Archaeological Site, Jerusalem*

She had come to Jerusalem anyway.

The limestone cave exhaled morning coolness as Dr. Sarah Davidson knelt in the dust, her trowel striking pottery with that distinctive sound—ceramic against tool, archaeology in motion. The first day of real excavation, and already every shard felt like a small victory.

"Another storage jar," she called across the cave to Father Michael Kowalski, who was hunched over his documentation station like a medieval scribe. The Jesuit priest looked up from his pottery sherds, decades of scholarly precision evident in the way he adjusted his wire-rimmed glasses.

"Byzantine period?" he asked, not looking up from his careful measurements.

"Earlier, I think. Much earlier." Sarah brushed dirt from the fragment, feeling the texture under her fingertips. "This clay's different. Rougher. First century, maybe."

Her grandmother's rosary caught the morning light where it hung from her excavation kit, the carved bone beads from Kraków looking oddly out of place among GPS units and soil sample containers. Sarah's fingers found the worn groove where *Babcia's* thumb had smoothed the bone through seventy years of prayer.

"Babcia always said these hills held secrets," she murmured in Polish, then caught herself. Harvard colleagues didn't appreciate personal sentiment in archaeological contexts.

But Father Kowalski smiled, and something in his expression carried weight of experience beyond academic archaeology. "Your grandmother sounds wise. What else did she tell you about these hills?"

Sarah felt heat rise in her cheeks. She'd been away from the Church for years—why had she requested a Catholic archaeologist for this dig? "She used to say the Church was like Poland. That she survives everything because she has something the world can't kill."

"And what did you think of that?"

"At Harvard?" Sarah's laugh was bitter. "I learned to stop believing in things that can't be killed."

Father Kowalski set down his measuring tape and looked at her with the particular attention of someone who has learned to distinguish between questions people ask and questions people are actually asking."

The memory hit without warning: *Babcia's* papery hand cupping her cheek during that last visit before graduate school. "Sarenka," the old woman had whispered, "faith is not about understanding everything. Faith is about trusting what you cannot understand."

"But academia teaches the opposite," Sarah said, more to herself than to him.

Father Kowalski set down his measuring tape. "Does it? Or does it teach us to understand better so we can trust more deeply?"

## Professional Curiosity

Before Sarah could answer, her trowel struck something that wasn't pottery. Something harder. Denser.

"Father." Her voice was barely a whisper. "Come look at this."

The pottery vessel emerged from the soil like a time capsule, its contents wrapped in linen that should have disintegrated centuries ago. But the fabric held, and beneath it—

"Wood," Father Kowalski breathed, kneeling beside her. "Preserved wood."

Neither of them moved for a moment.

The linen was intact. Not fragmentary, not the ghost of fabric that usually survived two millennia in Palestinian soil — intact, with structure, the weave still legible under her brush. The oils that had preserved it were still present, sharp and resinous in the cool cave air. Sarah had worked in Palestinian archaeology for six years. She knew what organic material looked like at two thousand years: powder, stain, the faint chemistry of what had been. Not this. Not a vessel you could still call a vessel, wrapping you could still call wrapping.

She sat back on her heels and looked at Kowalski.

He was looking at the linen. His expression was the one she would come to recognize over the following months — not wonder exactly, more like a man confirming something he had half believed for a long time and was not sure he was prepared to have confirmed.

Sarah's hands trembled as she brushed soil from the ancient wrapping. The aromatic oils still clung to the fabric, their scent sharp and unmistakable even after nearly two millennia.

"This is impossible," she whispered. "This level of preservation... Father, this violates everything we know about organic matter degradation in Palestinian soil conditions."

"Unless," he said quietly, "it was preserved intentionally. By people who understood its significance." His voice carried the same certainty she'd heard from relief workers in dangerous places—those who had learned to recognize the miraculous when they encountered it.

## Personal Faith

Sarah looked up from the fragments, meeting his eyes. "What are you suggesting?"

"I'm suggesting," Father Kowalski said quietly, "that we've just discovered something early Christians considered sacred enough to preserve across twenty centuries. People have given everything to protect fragments like these. That alone tells us something about what they believed they were protecting."

The cave fell silent except for the distant sound of Jerusalem traffic. Sarah stared at the fragments in her hands, their presence challenging every materialist assumption she'd built her career upon.

"Father, I need to tell you something." The words came out in a rush. "I wasn't entirely honest in my application. I was raised Catholic—Notre Dame undergraduate—but I've been away from the Church for years. I don't know why I requested a priest for this dig."

His smile was gentle, understanding. "Perhaps because some part of you sensed what we might find here."

"Or because I'm intellectually compromised by religious bias, just like my Harvard colleagues suspect."

"Sarah." His use of her first name made her look up. "What if intellectual honesty means following evidence wherever it leads, even when it challenges our assumptions?"

## The Unusual Preservation

She looked down at the fragments again, their impossible preservation mocking decades of scientific training. "What if we're wrong? What if this is just... wood?"

"Then we document what we found and publish our methodology. But what if we're right?"

The question hung in the morning air like incense. Sarah felt something shifting inside her, some barrier she'd built between faith and reason beginning to crack.

"If we're right," she said slowly, "then everything changes."

"Everything," he agreed. "The question is: are you prepared for that?"

Sarah held the fragments up to the light filtering through the cave entrance. Somewhere in the distance, Jerusalem's church bells announced the hour, their bronze voices carrying across stone and time.

"I don't know," she admitted. "But I think *Babcia* would tell me that not knowing is where faith begins."

## The Journey Begins

Father Kowalski began packing his equipment with the efficiency of decades in the field. "We need to get these to Rome. Cardinal Bertoli has arranged access to quantum analysis equipment at the Pontifical Institute — the kind that can read environmental history from organic matter at the molecular level."

"How far beyond?"

He paused. "If these fragments hold what I think they hold, we will need every instrument we have. And then some."

As they carefully wrapped the fragments for transport, Sarah felt her grandmother's presence as clearly as if the old woman were standing beside her in the cave. *Faith is not about understanding everything,* the memory whispered. *Faith is about trusting what you cannot understand.*

For the first time in years, Sarah thought she might be ready to trust.

She kept her hand on the wrapping a moment longer, then stood and carried the fragments toward the light.
