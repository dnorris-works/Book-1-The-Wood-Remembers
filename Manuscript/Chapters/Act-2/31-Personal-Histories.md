# Chapter 31: Personal Histories

## Sarah's Background

*January 12, 2025 - Afternoon - Jerusalem Excavation Site*

The afternoon heat had driven them deeper into the limestone cave, where centuries of Christian pilgrims had carved prayers into stone walls. Sarah sat on a camp stool, sharing her field lunch with Father Kowalski while the fragments rested safely in their protective case nearby.

"You know," she said, unwrapping a sandwich, "I keep thinking about my father."

"Tell me about him."

"Deacon William Davidson. Forty years at St. Stanislaus parish in South Bend." Sarah's voice caught. "Everyone loved him. The funeral Mass was standing room only."

Father Kowalski waited, recognizing the space between words where grief lived.

"Mom got pancreatic cancer when I was finishing my doctoral coursework," Sarah continued. "Dad held it together through the chemo, the surgeries, all of it. But near the end..."

The specific memory she carried of him was from a Sunday afternoon when she was nine. He had been refinishing the kitchen table — the old one, the one her grandmother had brought over from Poland — and he had let her help sand the edges. He guided her hand the whole time, his palm over her palm, showing her how to go with the grain. When they were done, he held her up so she could see the whole surface in the slant of the window light. "See how it comes back?" he said. "It was always this good. It just needed someone to look for it."

She could still see him standing in the ICU hallway after the oncologist had delivered the final verdict. Dad in his clerical collar, his hands shaking.

"He looked at me and said, 'Sarah, I've spent forty years preaching about God's love. How do I explain to my parishioners why God is letting your mother suffer like this?'"

"What did you tell him?"

"I was twenty-six and thought I knew everything. I told him maybe the questions were more honest than the answers." Sarah's laugh was bitter. "God, I was so arrogant."

"That doesn't sound arrogant."

"By Easter, Dad had stopped saying Mass. Stopped preaching. The man who'd taught me the Hail Mary was sitting in our kitchen at 3 AM, telling me he wasn't sure he believed in anything anymore."

Father Kowalski was quiet for a long moment. "How did that affect your faith?"

"It destroyed it." The words came out flat, matter-of-fact. "If a man that good, that devoted, could lose his faith watching his wife die, then what chance did the rest of us have?"

"Is that why you chose Harvard over Catholic graduate programs?"

Sarah nodded. "I wanted scholarship without all the... complications. Clean materialism. No mystery, no suffering without explanation. Just evidence and logic."

"And how's that working out?"

Sarah gestured toward the fragments. "Well, I'm sitting in a cave in Jerusalem holding wood that shouldn't exist, talking to a Jesuit priest about quantum consciousness. So... not great."

## Father Kowalski's Journey

Father Kowalski smiled. "Sarah, can I tell you about my crisis?"

"You had one?"

"El Salvador. 1987. I was thirty-four, fresh out of theology, assigned to document testimonies from families of the disappeared."

His voice took on a weight Sarah hadn't heard before.

"There was a woman, Maria Gonzalez. She pressed her eight-year-old son's bloodstained shirt into my hands. Just... thrust it at me." He paused. "'Padre,' she said, 'where is God when innocent blood cries out?'"

Sarah watched his face as the memory played out.

"Forty years of Jesuit training, Sarah. Philosophy, theology, spiritual formation. And I sat in that woman's kitchen, surrounded by pictures of her murdered child, with absolutely nothing to say."

"What happened?"

"I went back to the provincial, Father Garmendia. Told him I was done. That I couldn't represent a God who let children die while their mothers watched." Father Kowalski took off his glasses, cleaning them with unnecessary care. "I expected him to try to talk me out of leaving."

"But he didn't?"

"He said something I've never forgotten: 'Miguel, your questions honor the children more than easy answers would. God doesn't need our certainty—He needs our faithfulness in the darkness.'"

Sarah felt something tighten in her chest. "That's... that's almost exactly what Dad's final letter said."

"Your father left you a letter?"

"Found it after the funeral. He wrote that in the end he had stopped trying to explain God to people and started trying to stay with them in the dark." Sarah's voice broke slightly. "I thought it meant he'd given up. But what if..."

"What if what?"

"What if he'd found something deeper than the faith he started with?"

Father Kowalski replaced his glasses. "Sarah, your father spent forty years offering answers to people in pain. Maybe watching your mother suffer taught him that being present in pain is more sacred than explaining it away."

"But he stopped saying Mass."

"Did he stop loving your mother?"

"No. If anything, more."

"Did he stop caring for his parishioners?"

Sarah thought about the funeral, the stories people told. "No. He just... he visited them differently. Sat with them more. Talked less."

"Maybe he discovered that love matters more than explanations."

## Scientific and Spiritual Intersections

They sat in silence, the fragments nearby like a presence waiting to be acknowledged.

"Michael," Sarah said finally, "what if these fragments really do contain... something? What if MEMORIA detects actual consciousness patterns from the crucifixion?"

"What scares you about that possibility?"

"Everything." The word came out as a whisper. "If I publish findings that suggest consciousness can be preserved in wood for two thousand years, my academic career is over. Harvard will label me a religious extremist. My peers will question every paper I've ever written."

"And if you don't publish them?"

"Then I spend the rest of my life knowing I chose professional safety over truth."

Father Kowalski reached into his pocket and pulled out a small wooden cross, worn smooth by decades of handling. "Olive wood from Gethsemane. A gift from my novice master."

Sarah examined it. "Do you believe it has special properties?"

"I believe it connects me to something larger than myself. Whether that's molecular memory or sacramental grace or just the power of human intention—does it matter?"

"To a scientist? Yes."

"To a person of faith?"

Sarah was quiet for a long moment. "I don't know what I am anymore."

"Maybe that's exactly where God needs you to be."

Father Kowalski waited. There was more weight in the silence than she'd accounted for.

"There was someone," Sarah said, before she'd decided to say it. "Before Harvard. Before any of this." She kept her voice even, the way she kept her voice even when she was reading data she didn't yet know what to do with. "We were close. He was Catholic — serious about it, the whole life. And then he decided the Church was calling him and that I wasn't."

She picked up her water bottle. Set it back down without drinking.

"He entered seminary." She looked at the olive wood cross in Kowalski's hand rather than at his face. "Daniel Reeves." A pause that had nothing comfortable in it. "I haven't heard from him since."

She said it the way she'd name a city she'd once lived in and left. The way you named something when the naming was the only part you could manage. Father Kowalski, with the restraint of a confessor, did not press it.

As afternoon shadows lengthened across the cave floor, Sarah felt her grandmother's influence more strongly than she had in years. *Babcia* had understood something about material objects carrying spiritual significance—not because of magic, but because of love.

"Michael, can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"Do you think my father would be proud of what we've found here?"

Father Kowalski's smile was gentle. "I think your father would be proud that you're asking better questions instead of settling for easy answers."

## Preparing for Rome

Sarah looked at the fragments one more time. "Then let's take them to Rome. Let's see what MEMORIA finds."

"Even if it costs you everything?"

"Especially if it costs me everything." Sarah stood up, feeling something she hadn't felt in years—not certainty, but trust. "Because if *Babcia* and Dad taught me anything, it's that the things worth having are usually the things that cost everything."

As they prepared to leave the cave, Sarah touched the rosary hanging from her kit. For the first time since graduate school, she said a prayer—not for answers, but for courage to live faithfully with whatever questions lay ahead.
