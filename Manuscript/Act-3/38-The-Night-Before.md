# Chapter 38: The Night Before

*March 11, 2025 — Pontifical Biblical Institute, Rome*

The email from Singapore had arrived at 4:15 that afternoon.

Father Kowalski had read it twice, replied with three careful sentences thanking Dr. Tanaka for his thoroughness and confirming he would relay the findings to Dr. Davidson in the morning, and then spent the next seven hours not thinking about it, which had required a great deal of effort.

The reading was scheduled for nine o'clock. Sarah would do what she always did — arrive early, move equipment with the focused quiet of someone who had learned to treat preparation as prayer. Chen would arrive exactly on time, coffee in hand, his skepticism worn down to something more honest than skepticism by now. They didn't need Kowalski in the lab for the reading itself. His job had been the access, the permissions, the eighteen months of careful institutional navigation that had placed two scientists and a collection of ancient wood inside a Vatican quantum lab. That work was done. Tomorrow would speak for itself.

He should sleep.

He turned off the desk lamp, sat in the dark for a moment, turned it back on.

The Singapore report was still open on his laptop. He'd minimized it and then, without meaning to, restored it again. He looked at the third paragraph for what he estimated was the ninth time.

*Coherence patterns confirmed across all nine fragment nodes. Signal strength within 2% of Vatican baseline readings. Methodological replication complete. The patterns are not artifact.*

Dr. Tanaka had added a handwritten note in the scan of his signed confirmation — outside the formal document, just a line at the bottom in careful English: *I don't know what this is. But it is real.*

Kowalski appreciated that. The honesty of it. A physicist in Singapore who had gone looking for an error and found instead that there was no error.

He closed the laptop.

The room was quiet in the particular way that institutional buildings were quiet at midnight — not silent, but emptied of purpose, the hum of ventilation systems filling a space that would be full of intention again by morning. He'd lived in rooms like this his entire adult life. Novitiate dormitories, Jesuit residences, guest quarters in universities and rectories across three continents. He was comfortable in this quiet. He had learned, long ago, to make it a kind of company.

He was not comfortable tonight.

He pulled his breviary toward him, found the office for the hour, and read the psalm. He read it again. The words sat on the surface of his mind without sinking in, the way words do when the mind is occupied underneath with something it hasn't found language for yet.

He closed the breviary.

El Salvador was thirty-eight years ago. He did not, as a rule, go back there deliberately. The memory had the quality of a healed bone — present in the structure of him, occasionally aching when the weather changed, but no longer the site of active injury. Father Garmendia's words had done that. *Your questions honor the children more than easy answers would.* He had carried that sentence through three and a half decades of ministry. He had offered versions of it to other priests in their own dark hours. It had become, without his fully deciding this, the architecture of his faith.

God doesn't need our certainty. He needs our faithfulness in the darkness.

He'd said it to Sarah in that cave in Jerusalem and watched something shift in her face, and he'd meant it, every word. He still meant it.

And yet.

He went to the window. The city below was nearly still — a few late cars, the amber wash of streetlights over old stone, the dome of a church visible above the roofline two blocks east. He had loved Rome from the first time he'd seen it. He loved the way antiquity and the present occupied the same space here without apology, each visible in the other. It suited him. It suited the kind of faith he'd arrived at, which was less about certainty than about patience — the long work of staying.

But Tanaka's note was still in his mind. *I don't know what this is. But it is real.*

If it was real — and it was real, the data was real, he had understood that for months — then the cross was real in a way that was not metaphor, not tradition, not the gathered weight of two thousand years of devotion lending significance to objects that were, materially, just wood. It was real the way Maria Gonzalez's son was real. The boy whose name Kowalski still knew: Andrés. Eight years old. The shirt she had pressed into his hands had been blue, a child's blue with a small collar, and there had been a handprint on it — not the boy's — that he had looked at and then looked away from and had been looking away from ever since.

*Padre. Where is God when innocent blood cries out?*

He had answered her, eventually. Not that day. He'd had nothing that day. But over thirty-eight years he had built an answer — the answer that Garmendia had handed him the frame for, that his own long ministry had gradually filled in: God is present in the darkness. God does not explain suffering. God enters it. The Cross is the proof not that suffering is prevented but that it is not suffered alone.

It was a good answer. He believed it. He had staked his life on it.

What he hadn't known, until this afternoon, was that the Cross might also be proof in the other sense — physical proof, empirical proof, the kind of proof that Tanaka could confirm and Chen could verify and that would be published in a peer-reviewed journal and read by physicists who would have to grapple with what it meant.

He had spent thirty-eight years saying *God doesn't need our certainty* and discovering, tonight, that some part of him had taken comfort in the uncertainty.

The faith he'd rebuilt after El Salvador was a faith that lived in the dark by choice — not because the light wasn't available but because waiting in the dark was itself the practice, the obedience, the act of trust that mattered. If the light came on, what happened to thirty-eight years of learning to wait for it?

And what about Maria Gonzalez? What did it mean that God had been present in that wood at the moment it was used, that consciousness had been encoded by the intensity of what happened there, that love — or whatever the physicists were calling it — had been strong enough to survive two millennia in the molecular structure of those fragments? What did it mean that all of that was true, and Andrés Gonzalez was still dead, and his mother had still sat in her kitchen with a shirt that still carried the mark of whoever had killed her child?

The presence in the wood didn't fix that. No reading would fix that.

Kowalski stood at the window a long time.

He wasn't losing his faith. He was clear about that, at least — this was not El Salvador, this was not the ground going out from under him. It was more like the ground solidifying unexpectedly under a foot he'd learned to place with care. He had built his theology around the absence of proof, around the nobility of believing without it, and now the proof was coming anyway, and it was asking him to think again about what he'd thought he'd understood.

*Where is God when innocent blood cries out?*

He had the same answer. He still had it. God is there. God enters it. The Cross is the evidence.

But the Cross was evidence in a new sense now, and that meant the question was also evidence, and he didn't know yet what to do with that. He didn't know if Maria Gonzalez would find comfort in it or if it would feel, to her, like a door opened on a room that still held everything she'd lost. He didn't know if he was the right person to tell her, or if telling her was even his to do.

He knew that tomorrow morning he would walk into the lab and stand beside Sarah while she held a sensor over a fragment of ancient wood and detected what had been there all along, and that he would probably feel the way you feel in a church when the liturgy suddenly lands after years of rote — not surprised by the theology but surprised by the fact of it, the weight it turned out to have when it stopped being only words.

He sat back down at the desk. Opened his breviary again.

This time he didn't try to read. He just held it.

After a while he put it down and took out a piece of paper — actual paper, not his laptop — and wrote a letter he had no plan to send: *Maria. I don't know if you're still in San Salvador. I don't know if you think about that afternoon the way I do. I think about Andrés. I have never stopped. I want you to know that.*

He stopped there. Folded the paper once and set it on the desk.

Outside, a church bell began to count midnight. He listened to all twelve strokes, sitting still in the lamplight while Rome finished its long day around him and the morning made its way, hour by quiet hour, closer.

He would be there for the reading. He would watch Sarah's hands on the sensor and Chen's eyes on the monitors and the data arriving in real time on screens designed to detect what Thomas Aquinas had only been able to write about by touch.

He didn't know what he was going to feel when the signal confirmed.

He was, he decided, going to let himself find out.
