# Chapter 44: Roman Welcome

## The Commission

*March 5, 2025 - Pontifical Biblical Institute, Rome*

The commission met in a room that smelled of old paper and new carpet, which told Sarah something about the institution's priorities. Ancient manuscripts in the corridors, practical furniture in the working rooms. Cardinal Bertoli stood when she came in, which surprised her. So did three of the eight people around the table. The other five were already looking at their copies of her preprint.

Bertoli introduced the commission in the order he'd seated them: Dr. Antonelli from particle physics, who had published on quantum decoherence in biological systems and had been, Bertoli mentioned carefully, "initially skeptical of the MEMORIA methodology." Sister Mary Catherine from archaeological chemistry, who'd been in the field for thirty years and had the hands to prove it. Father Kowalski on video from Jerusalem, his connection slightly degraded. Four others whose names Sarah registered and whose faces she read quickly: two were waiting to be convinced, one had already decided, one was watching her rather than the preprint.

Dr. Antonelli didn't wait for the pleasantries to fully resolve. "Dr. Davidson. Your decoherence baseline."

"Yes."

"You're using Liu and Zhang as the reference framework for expected coherence decay in lignin networks."

"Correct."

"Liu and Zhang worked with isolated molecular systems under laboratory conditions at near-absolute zero. Your fragments spent fourteen centuries in Palestinian limestone at ambient temperature." He looked up from the preprint. "Your baseline assumes comparable decoherence rates. That assumption is doing a great deal of work in your statistical argument, and I don't think it holds."

The room was quiet.

Sarah had prepared for this. She'd spent three days on the plane and in her hotel room preparing for exactly this, because Antonelli had published a paper eighteen months ago that she'd read four times and disagreed with on two points, and she had known he would be in this room.

"You're right that the baseline is imperfect," she said. "Liu and Zhang is the best available reference for lignin decoherence, and it's not the right reference. I say that in the paper. What I also say is that the anomaly we detected is large enough that even accounting for the baseline error by a factor of ten, the coherence patterns remain statistically impossible under any decoherence model currently in the literature."

"Factor of ten is generous."

"It's deliberately conservative. Run it at a factor of twenty. The conclusion doesn't change."

Antonelli wrote something on his copy. Not dismissively — the writing of someone actually doing the arithmetic. "I'll look at that."

"I'd welcome it. If I've made an error in the baseline construction I want to know."

He looked up. Something shifted slightly in his assessment of her. "The fractal dimension analysis. 1.73."

"Yes."

"Hausdorff dimension in that range appears in biological neural encoding. You're proposing this as evidence of experiential memory structure."

"I'm proposing it as consistent with experiential memory structure. I'm not claiming it as proof."

"What would proof look like?"

The question landed in the room and stayed there. Sarah had thought about this more than any other question.

"Decoding," she said. "Moving from structure to content. Reading what's stored rather than detecting that something is stored. We're nowhere near that. We may never be there. What we have is a structural argument that something was encoded — not what."

Antonelli set down his pen. He looked at her for a long moment, not hostile now but not satisfied either — the look of a man who has heard a good answer and doesn't trust it precisely because it's good. "You're telling me you've detected the architecture of memory in two-thousand-year-old wood and your evidence for that claim is that you can't decode it."

"I'm telling you that the structure is consistent with memory architecture and inconsistent with every alternative explanation I've been able to model. Including yours." She pulled up the supplementary data on her laptop and turned the screen toward him. "Your 2023 paper on decoherence in aged organic matrices — your own Hausdorff values for non-information-bearing cellulose cluster between 1.84 and 1.96. We're at 1.73. That's not baseline error. That's a different category of structure."

Antonelli reached across and tilted the screen toward himself. He studied it for a moment. Tilted it back. "You used my paper to calibrate your anomaly threshold."

"Yes."

"You didn't cite it."

"It's in the supplementary references. Footnote eleven."

He found it. Read the footnote. Wrote something on his copy again — longer this time. When he looked up his expression had changed in a way she couldn't quite read: not won over, but rearranged. "The baseline problem is real," he said. "But I think you know that."

"I do. I said so in the paper."

Sister Mary Catherine spoke from the far end. "Dr. Davidson, I've worked in field archaeology for thirty years. Provenance is everything. Walk me through your chain of custody from excavation to analysis."

Sarah walked her through it. It took fifteen minutes. Sister Mary Catherine interrupted four times with precise questions about containment protocols and cross-contamination risk. By the end she was satisfied with two of Sarah's answers and still holding one open.

"The resin seal on the primary container," she said. "Applied at the excavation site before Vatican custody. Who applied it?"

"Father Kowalski."

Both women looked at the screen. Kowalski's connection resolved enough to see his expression. "I applied it," he said. "I can provide the documentation. The seal integrity was verified by Vatican conservation on receipt."

"I'll want the verification log."

"I'll send it this afternoon."

Sister Mary Catherine made a note. Not disapproval — diligence. The kind that had kept her career clean for thirty years.

The session ran two hours. Bertoli asked no questions, which Sarah registered as its own kind of statement — he was letting his commission do the work without steering them. Dr. Santos raised the replication problem, which she answered the same way she'd answered it at Harvard: honestly, with full acknowledgment of the limitation. The difference was that Santos heard the honesty and moved on instead of using it as a lever.

Father Kowalski went last. "Sarah. One question."

"Yes."

"When you didn't know what you'd found — in the cave, the first day — what did you do?"

She thought about it. "I documented exactly what I observed. I didn't interpret until I had methodology to interpret with."

"That's the answer," he said, to the room as much as to her. The connection cut out and came back a second later, his face pixelated and then resolving. "That's the answer."

Bertoli thanked the commission and asked them to submit written questions within forty-eight hours. They filed out. Antonelli paused at the door.

"The baseline problem is real," he said. "But I think you know that." He left before she could respond.

She wasn't sure whether that was criticism or respect. Possibly both.

Bertoli walked with her to the building's entrance. The afternoon was cold, the courtyard empty except for a maintenance worker crossing it with unhurried purpose.

"That was honest engagement," she said.

"We have a lot to lose if we're wrong." He said it simply, without apology. "So do you."

"Yes."

"Good. Then we're working from the same place."

Her phone buzzed as she reached the street. A text from his office, already sent: *Dr. Davidson — the Holy Father has asked to be briefed on your work at your convenience. No urgency. When you're ready.*

She read it twice. Put her phone in her pocket and kept walking.
