# Chapter 9: The Waiting Cross

## The Yard

*33 CE - Passover Week*

There were other crosses in the yard.

Five of them, different sizes, different ages of wood. Two were older than me by the look of their surfaces — grey and checked at the ends where weather had worked the grain. One was newer, its wood still showing yellow heartwood at the cut surfaces, not yet fully cured. We stood together in the storage area in the posture of objects that have been made ready and are waiting for the use they were made for.

The yard was inside the city walls. I had never been inside the walls before. The sound was different: more enclosed, sound reflecting off stone rather than dissipating into open air. The smell was different too — stone dust and animal waste and the particular density of many thousands of people in close proximity, the smell that a city makes when it is full beyond its normal capacity.

Passover. I had heard the word on my hillside for over a hundred years, heard it with the anticipation that the pilgrims carried in their voices when they used it. Now I was inside the city during the thing itself, and I could understand why they anticipated it — the city was alive in a way that was not its ordinary life. Voices everywhere, music from different quarters at different hours, the specific smell of the roasting lamb that came on the Passover eve and stayed in the air for two days.

The cross selection happened on the third day of the feast.

Two soldiers came to the yard in the morning and walked the row of crosses with the same assessing gaze that the carpenter had brought to my hillside weeks before. They measured with their eyes and their hands — pressing the wood at the joint, checking the grain, standing back to assess the uprights. They moved along the row from the oldest to the newest and back again, conferring.

They stopped at me.

One of them pressed his palm flat against my upright and held it there. I had noticed this over the weeks — the way certain people's hands, when they rested against me, stayed longer than assessment required. He was not measuring anything when he did this. He was just present to the wood.

He said something to his companion. The other man looked at the joint where crossbeam met upright, checked the surfaces, nodded.

They marked me with chalk.

I would know this man again. Not because I could see his face, but because I know the quality of a hand that touches wood and stays. He would be the one still standing at the base of the hill when everyone else had gone, and he would be the one who said the thing that was true.

## The Arsenal

They came back the next morning with ropes and a cart. The loading was straightforward — ropes around the middle of the upright, men lifting the crossbeam end while others guided the base. The lateral press of the ropes was a new sensation: compression from outside rather than the internal pressure of wind and weather that I had known for over a hundred years. I felt my grain take the load and distribute it, the joint holding.

The cart moved through the city. The streets were narrow and the cross was long and progress was stop-and-start, the men calling ahead and then waiting while traffic cleared. I was horizontal on the cart, which gave me a changed view of the city: walls and gates and the underside of overhanging upper stories, the feet of people pressed to the sides of the street to let the cart through, faces looking down from windows.

The arsenal was near the Antonia Fortress on the north side of the Temple Mount. The soldiers there received the cross without ceremony and carried it inside.

Stone walls on four sides, a stone ceiling, the smell of iron and old leather and oil and the particular cold of a place that does not see direct sun. Other equipment was stored here — armour, weapons, the organized tools of occupation. I was leaned against a wall in an area near the door with two others already there.

This was the first time I had been indoors.

The city noise came through the stone walls reduced and flattened. I could hear Jerusalem out there — the Passover crowds, the Temple trumpets at the hours of prayer — but from inside the stone walls it all arrived as a single low pressure, the way sound arrives from very far away. I had spent my whole life vertical, reaching upward, and now the ceiling was above me and the floor held my weight and there was nothing to reach toward.

I was not afraid. I am not sure fear is what I mean. But I noticed the difference.

## The Days and the Night

Four days passed in that chamber. I counted them by the light in the narrow windows set high in the wall — rectangular strips that moved across the floor as the day turned and vanished entirely at night.

The city below the fortress grew louder each day as the pilgrims arrived for the festival. Jerusalem took in tens of thousands for Passover. I could feel their presence through the stone floor the way I had once felt roots moving through the hillside, a general pressure distributed across everything.

A soldier came through the storage chamber each morning to check the materials. He spent more time near my section than the rest. The second morning he ran his hand along my grain without looking around to see if anyone was watching. His hand was rough-palmed and warm. He didn't say anything. After a moment he moved on.

I thought about the hillside. I thought about the specific light in the hour before dawn when Jerusalem's walls caught the first grey rising and the dew on my leaves made the air taste clean. I thought about the night I held Miriam above the dogs, the way the stars looked from that position, the cold coming up from the ground and the weight of her pressed into my branches.

That was a different kind of weight than what was coming. I understood that, though I did not know the shape of what was coming. I could feel it the way I had always felt a particular storm before it arrived — not the content, just the size of it, the quality of the pressure building in the air.

On the last night, something changed in the city.

It began late — past the deepest hour, when the festival fires had burned low and most of the sound had settled. A disturbance, distant at first, voices moving fast through streets, the kind of purposeful movement that has a destination. Then torches visible through the high windows, not the steady glow of street fires but moving light, organized, heading somewhere.

The soldier who slept in the corridor outside the storage room woke. I heard him get up, heard him speak to someone, heard a reply he didn't seem to expect. More footsteps after that, several of them, going in the same direction.

The city quieted eventually. Not the peaceful quiet of the night settling — a different quiet. The quiet of something completed.

Then the fortress itself began to change. Not slowly, the way a building wakes with the morning, but all at once — the building waking up in coordinated ways rather than the loose patterns of a night watch. Torches in the corridors. The clank and shift of armour being adjusted. More messengers, moving with the quick purposeful step of someone carrying something urgent in one direction only.

Something had been decided.

The cold off the stone floor had settled into my grain through four nights. Cut wood loses its living warmth differently than a living tree — there is no sap moving, no slow interior metabolism to generate heat from below. I was cold all the way through. The iron smell of the chamber had become simply the smell of the air.

Then actual light came through the windows. Not grey — pale gold, the specific colour of early morning in spring before the sun clears the hills to the east. I had watched that light from my hillside every morning for over a century. From this angle, through two narrow slots in the stone, it looked the same.

The same light. The same sun. A completely different day.

## The Lifting

They came for me when the light was still new.

Four men — I knew by the footsteps entering the chamber, the pause while they oriented, then the approach toward my section. They did not speak much. The work of moving large timber is physical and they had done it before. Ropes around the cross at two points, lifting from the floor, the shock of being raised from horizontal after four days pressed flat against the stone.

Something redistributed when I was upright. Settled toward what had been my base, the heaviest part of me finding down again. The grain remembering which direction was which.

They carried me through the corridor and out through the fortress gates into open air.

The air hit me the way it always had — I had not expected to feel it so strongly, having been inside for only four days, but the difference was immediate and complete. Cool, moving, carrying the smell of the city and the morning and underneath all of it the particular dry scent of Jerusalem limestone warming in the early sun. I had been breathing stone and iron for four days. This was like drinking after thirst I had not known I had.

The sky above was pale and enormous.

I could see the Temple from here, its western face catching the morning sun, the gold on the roof blazing the way it had blazed every morning I could remember. The same view I had from the hillside, though at a different angle, closer, the walls of the city all around instead of at a distance.

The crowd I had heard through the stone was visible now — gathered at the far end of the open space, their backs to me, facing the governor's hall. Too far to read faces. The low continued sound of many people holding a position and waiting.

The soldiers formed up around me. The morning moved.

I did not know his face yet. I would know it in an hour.
