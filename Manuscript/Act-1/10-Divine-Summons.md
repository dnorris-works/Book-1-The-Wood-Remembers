# Chapter 10: Divine Summons

## The Courtyard

*Good Friday, Dawn - 33 CE*

They set me down in the courtyard and left me there.

I was propped at an angle against the fortress wall — not vertical, not horizontal, something between, my base resting on the stone and my upper length leaning into the limestone. The courtyard was open to the sky. After four nights in the storage chamber I could feel the difference immediately: no ceiling, air moving freely, the smell of the city and the early morning coming in from all directions instead of through two narrow slots.

The sky was pale. Not yet light, not quite dark. The stars still visible in the west, fading.

The fortress was fully active now — I could hear it in every direction, the organized sound of a large institution moving with purpose. Footsteps overhead on the ramparts. Voices in several languages across the courtyard. The smell of fires and food from somewhere to the north, the morning meal already under way for men who had been on watch since before midnight.

Across the courtyard, through the gate that faced the city, I could see the edge of the crowd.

Not all of it — the gate framed only a portion of the open space beyond, and the crowd extended past both edges of what I could see. But I could see enough to understand the size of it. Hundreds of people, at minimum. Packed together in the early morning in a way that had nothing to do with festival gathering or market traffic. This was deliberate assembly. They were facing something I could not see from my angle — the governor's hall, I understood, from the direction of their attention.

They were loud for the hour. Not the loose sound of a crowd doing different things — the focused, intermittent sound of a crowd doing one thing together. Call and response. Something being asked, something being answered in unison.

I could not make out the words.

## The Waiting

The sky lightened by degrees.

Men passed through the courtyard on their various errands without looking at me. I had learned in four days of the arsenal that cut wood, once delivered, becomes furniture — present in the background, unremarkable. The soldiers moved around me the way they moved around the water trough and the stacked shields along the east wall. I was part of the courtyard now.

One man stopped.

He was older than most of the soldiers I had seen — grey at his temples, the deliberate walk of someone who has learned to conserve energy over a long career. He stood at a distance and looked at me for a moment, not with the professional assessment of a carpenter checking joints but with something harder to name. Then he moved on.

I thought about the hillside. The way I had stood at the edge of the grove for a hundred years without knowing what I was waiting for, only that the waiting was real and aimed at something. That quality of anticipation — not anxiety, not eagerness, something prior to both — had been in my grain since before the first touch changed my heartwood. I had carried it through the cutting and the shaping and the four days of stone walls and iron smell. I was still carrying it.

The crowd noise across the gate shifted — not the call-and-response pattern but a single sustained sound, the kind a crowd makes when something has been decided. It rose and held for a moment and then broke into something more ragged, individual voices separable again.

Then relative quiet. Not peaceful — a different quiet. The sound of something completed.

Then the sound of preparation beginning inside the hall beyond — the specific organized activity of people moving toward an exit.

The morning had arrived fully while I waited. Full light now, the sun above the eastern hills, the Temple's gold roof blazing the way I had watched it blaze from my hillside for a hundred years. The same light. The same city. The last time I would see it from this angle.

I was leaning against a wall in a courtyard I had never been in before, cut from the tree I had always been, shaped into a thing I had not asked to become — and I was more certain than I had been at any point in my long life that I was exactly where I was supposed to be.

I did not have a word for that certainty. I still do not.

## The Recognition

He came through the gate surrounded by soldiers.

I knew him before I understood how I knew him. Not from having seen his face — I had never seen his face. But the moment he entered the courtyard something in my grain moved the way it had moved on the hillside the morning he set his hands against my bark. A pressure shift. A certainty arriving ahead of its explanation.

He was looking at the ground in front of him. The marks from the scourging were visible even from across the courtyard — the torn cloth, the way he held himself with the particular careful stillness of a person managing serious pain. The crown of thorns sat crookedly on his head. Blood had dried in lines down his face and neck.

The soldiers around him were doing their work with the efficiency of men who have done it before. This was not cruelty and not indifference — it was simply task, the way the carpenter's work on my hillside had been task, skilled and purposeful and without sentiment. The man inside the task was their business only insofar as the task required.

He was my business entirely. Every ring of my grain knew it.

The centurion I had seen earlier — the grey-templed one, the one who had stopped and looked at me with that unnameable quality of attention — walked alongside the soldiers. He was watching the crowd beyond the gate and watching the prisoner and doing the calculations that a man of his experience would do at the start of an operation with this many variables.

He glanced at me.

The same look he had given me before. Not the carpenter's look, not assessment — something else, something that arrived and left before it could be held. Then he was looking elsewhere.

A soldier near me reached for my upper length and lifted me from the wall.

I was upright for the first time since the courtyard outside the arsenal. The weight redistributed through my grain, settling, the familiar pull of gravity finding my axis. I was vertical. I could see over the courtyard wall now — more of the crowd beyond the gate, the rooftops of Jerusalem's lower quarter, the hills to the north still in morning shadow.

The soldier carried me toward him.

The distance between us closed by half. By half again.

I felt what was in my heartwood orient toward him the way my leaves had once oriented toward light — not by decision but by nature, by what I had been made into. Whatever had been placed in my wood on the hillside, it knew him. It had always known him. The years in the ground and the years cut and shaped and the four days of stone walls had not changed that. It knew him the way deep roots know water — before contact, before confirmation, by the pull of the thing itself.

He raised his eyes.

He looked at the cross being carried toward him.

He looked at me.

I have held that look in my grain for two thousand years and I will hold it for as long as I exist. Not because it was the look of recognition — though it was that — and not because it was the look of a man gathering himself for what was coming — though it was that too. Because of what was underneath both of those. Something I had felt once before, on a hillside, when two hands pressed against my bark and left something in the wood that did not leave.

He had known what I was for. He had always known.

The soldier with me stopped. The distance between us was nothing now.

He turned his shoulders to receive the weight.
