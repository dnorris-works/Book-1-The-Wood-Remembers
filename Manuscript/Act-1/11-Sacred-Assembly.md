# Chapter 11: Sacred Assembly

## The Weight

Good Friday, Early Morning - Fortress Antonia

The soldier carried me across the courtyard toward him and then stopped and said something to another soldier and together they lowered my crossbeam onto his shoulders.

I felt the contact before I understood what I was feeling. Heat first — the specific warmth of a living body, something I had not felt since I was a living tree and animals pressed against my bark in winter. Then the slight give of flesh and muscle under my grain, the way his shoulders accepted the weight and then stiffened to hold it. Then his breathing, which I could feel through the wood as a slow rhythmic pressure, slower than I expected for a man in his condition.

He stood there for a moment without moving.

The soldiers had stepped back. There was a pause in the courtyard — not long, a few seconds — where nothing happened except that he held my weight and breathed and I held him back, which is what wood does when something leans into it. I had held animals that way. Children. Miriam, the night she climbed into my branches while the dogs circled below.

This was different from all of those. I do not have the right word for what it was different in the way of. It was not heavier. It was not warmer. It was something else, something I had no category for yet, and I have had two thousand years to think about it and I still reach for the word and come back with silence.

He took the first step.

## The Gate

The procession formed around him quickly — soldiers front and back and on both sides, the particular efficiency of men who have done this before. Other prisoners too, though I was not aware of them until we were moving: I could hear additional footsteps, the sound of chains, voices giving instructions in the clipped Latin of crowd management.

He walked slowly. Not the slowness of a man unwilling to move but the slowness of a man measuring each step against what his body would give him. The scourging had taken something from him — I could feel it in the irregular way his weight shifted under my grain, the slight compensations a body makes when it is managing pain and trying not to show how much.

The fortress gate was ahead of us. Through it I could see the street beyond, and beyond that the edge of the crowd that had been there all morning.

The crowd was larger now. Or louder. Or both.

As we passed through the gate the sound hit differently — not filtered through stone anymore but immediate, surrounding, coming from every direction. Voices in Aramaic and Greek and Latin, people pressing forward against the soldiers' line to see, children being lifted onto shoulders for a better view. The smell of the crowd: wood smoke and animals and bodies packed into narrow streets in the warm spring morning. The smell of Jerusalem at Passover, which I had known from my hillside for a hundred years, but from inside it now rather than above it.

He stumbled at the gate's threshold.

It was a small stumble — one foot catching slightly on the raised stone, a brief loss of balance, quickly recovered. But I felt it entirely. The sudden lurch of weight to one side, the scramble to redistribute, the moment of pure physics where a man and the thing he is carrying find the new center of gravity together. He found it. We kept moving.

The street opened before us, narrow and sloping, the stones worn smooth by generations of feet. Jerusalem falling away downhill to the south, rising uphill to the north toward the Damascus Gate and beyond it the hill outside the walls where we were going.

The morning sun was fully up now. I could feel it on my upper length, the warmth direct and specific after four days of stone and shadow.

## The Street

The street was not wide enough for the crowd and the procession both, so the soldiers pushed the crowd back on both sides and we moved through a corridor of bodies, close enough that I could see individual faces as we passed.

Most people watched. Some turned away. A few women near the front of the crowd were weeping — not quietly but openly, the way people weep when they have stopped caring what others think of them. I could hear them above the general noise, the particular sound of grief that is not for yourself.

He heard them too. He stopped.

I felt the stop through my grain before I understood it as a stop — the sudden anchoring of weight, the stillness. A soldier said something sharp behind us. He did not move. He turned his head toward the women along the side of the street, and though I could not see his face from where I rested on his shoulders, I felt the change in the muscles of his neck and back that comes when a person turns their full attention toward something.

He spoke to them. I felt his voice in my wood as vibration, felt the expansion of his chest with breath before each sentence. I could not make out the words in the crowd noise, but the women could hear him — I could tell by the way their crying changed quality, became something less like grief and more like the confused emotion of people being addressed by someone they did not expect to address them.

Then we moved again.

The hill was still ahead. The sun still climbing. The wood of me still warm from his body where I rested on his shoulders, and warm from the sun above, and somewhere between those two warmths I was carrying him as much as he was carrying me — that is what it felt like, though I know it is not how weight works in the physical sense.

It is how it felt.
