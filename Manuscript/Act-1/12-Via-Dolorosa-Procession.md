# Chapter 12: Via Dolorosa - Procession

## The Street

Good Friday, Mid-Morning - Via Dolorosa

The street narrowed after the first turn.

The crowd pressed in from both sides — not hostile exactly, but dense, the way crowds at festival time pack themselves into whatever space the soldiers leave them. I could feel it through him: the way his body braced slightly each time a sound came too close from the left or right, the involuntary tightening across his shoulders that transmitted itself into my grain. He was managing many things at once. The weight of me. The pain from the scourging. The footing on uneven stones. The crowd.

He had spoken to the women. That was behind us now, two turns back. I did not know what he had said — the words were absorbed into the crowd noise before they reached me clearly — but I had felt the quality of his attention shift toward them, felt him gather something and give it, and then we moved again.

The street rose on a gentle grade. Uphill to the north, then uphill again. The hill outside the walls was still perhaps half a Roman mile ahead. His pace had slowed since we left the fortress courtyard — not dramatically, not enough that the soldiers had intervened, but I could feel it in the rhythm of each step. Shorter. More deliberate. Each footfall a decision.

A child's voice somewhere in the crowd, asking something. A woman's answer, quick and hushed.

The Temple was visible above the rooflines when the street opened briefly at a crossing — the gold of the roof catching the midmorning sun. Same view I had looked at from my hillside for a hundred years. From down here in the city streets, with bodies on all sides and the smell of the crowd and his breathing in my wood, it looked like something from another world.

## Simon

The fall came at the crossing.

Not dramatic — he went to one knee, the movement controlled even in exhaustion, one hand catching the pavement before he was fully down. My weight shifted hard to the left and for a moment we were both at an angle, the cross tilted, soldiers stepping in from two sides.

He pushed himself back up. It took longer than the first time.

A soldier scanned the crowd and pointed at a man standing near the front — large, travel-dusty, the look of someone who had walked a long distance to get here. The soldier said something in Latin. The man shook his head, not aggressively, the head-shake of someone who does not understand why he is being spoken to. The soldier said it again. Another soldier reached into the crowd and took hold of the man's arm.

He was African, from the bearing and the color of his skin — Cyrene, I would later understand, a city on the north coast of Africa where a large Jewish community had settled over the generations. He had come to Jerusalem for Passover. He had stood at the edge of the crowd to see what was happening in the street, and now a soldier had his arm.

They brought him forward and positioned him behind me, his hands going to my lower length. His grip was strong — the hands of someone who worked with his body — but uncertain, a man learning a new weight mid-motion. He adjusted twice before he found the right position.

Then the three of us were moving again.

## Two Pairs of Hands

The difference was immediate and strange.

His hands — the hands I had been feeling since the courtyard — were at my upper length near the crossbeam joint. I knew them now. They were warm in the particular way of a body running hot from pain and effort, the grip steady despite everything, the pressure even. There was something in the contact of his hands that I have no ordinary word for. Not comfort exactly. Not meaning exactly. Something prior to both of those.

Simon's hands were at my lower length. Strong, as I said. The grip of a man who knew physical work. But different — the uncertainty of a stranger learning a shared task, adjusting constantly to redistribute load, the hands of someone who did not know yet what was needed of him and was figuring it out in real time.

I felt both at once. The known and the unknown. The certain and the discovering.

The weight between the three of us redistributed differently now — less of it concentrated at that single point on his shoulders, more spread across the length of me, Simon bearing the lower mass while he bore the upper. His breathing changed. Slightly slower. The bracing in his shoulder muscles reduced.

Simon said nothing at first. His breathing was the quick breathing of someone managing unexpected weight. He found his footing on the stones. He matched pace. After perhaps fifty steps he was moving smoothly, the adjustment made, his body having solved the problem his mind had still been working on.

The crowd watched the three of us — the condemned man, the African stranger, and the wood between them — move up through the narrowing street toward the gate in the northern wall. I do not know what it looked like from the outside. From the inside it felt like something that had found its shape.

The hill was ahead. The sun was high enough now that I could feel it directly on my upper surface, warm and consistent, the same sun I had turned toward for a hundred and twenty years.

Simon's hands held steady.

We kept moving.
