# Chapter 8: The Woodcutters

## The Order

He came up the slope alone the evening before, in the last hour of light, and I knew his step before I saw him.

I had recorded it not long before, on the morning he walked away from my hillside and left Miriam and Benjamin at my roots. The specific weight of a man who moves with authority — not the authority of rank, though he had that, but the authority of a person who has decided what everything is for before he arrives at it. Decimus Varro. Centurion. The man who had tied Levi to my trunk and the man who had ordered the soldiers down the hill when there was nothing left to do.

He stood at the edge of my shade and looked at me the way he had looked at me before: not at a tree but at a problem that had not yet been solved to his satisfaction.

The stone circle was still at my base. Miriam had been adding to it for twenty years. No one had moved the stones after the soldiers left that morning. They were still there, arranged with the precision of a girl who had cared about the arrangement, nested into the ground as if they belonged to it.

Varro looked at the stones.

He walked around my trunk slowly, the way the assessors had walked the hillside in the earlier years, checking sight lines and angles. Then he stopped and looked toward Jerusalem, visible over the slope in the last of the evening light, the Temple roof catching the gold the way it always did at this hour.

I have watched human beings make decisions for over a hundred and twenty years. I know the difference between a man solving a practical problem and a man making a point. The practical problem does not require a man to stand and look at the city before he gives an order. The point does.

He turned and looked at me again. Then he called down to the road below, where two soldiers were waiting, and gave them instructions I could not fully parse — only the shape of the words, the flat coordinating tone of a man specifying something, and the name of a carpenter, and a time.

Before dawn.

He came back to the base of my trunk and stood there for a moment in the failing light. He looked at the stones once more. He said nothing. His boot moved one of Miriam's stones — not a kick, a deliberate displacement, a small editorial — and then he went back down the slope.

I have thought about that displaced stone for two thousand years. It was such a small gesture. He had ordered men killed on this hillside and walked away from the bodies and organized the soldiers efficiently and given commands in a level voice. He did not need to move the stone. He moved it because he wanted to. Because the circle had been a statement and he wanted to make a counter-statement and one displaced stone was sufficient for his purposes.

Olive trees are not used for crosses.

I had watched the timber trade on the road below for over a century. I knew what wood Rome chose for this work and why. They brought pine down from the forests north of Galilee — fast-growing, straight-grained, cheap, plentiful. Sometimes cypress. Cedar when it was available. Wood that costs nothing to fell and nothing to waste, wood from groves that no one has tended for generations and no one will mourn. Wood that carries no meaning to the people who will watch a man hang from it.

An olive tree is not that kind of wood.

An olive tree in Palestine is a family's inheritance. It takes forty years to come to full production. A tree my age — a hundred and twenty years, roots deep in limestone, pressing oil that had fed generations on this road — was worth more than most men earned in a decade. You do not fell an olive tree for lumber. You do not use olive wood for crosses. The grain is too dense, too twisted from a century of growth, too costly to shape and too slow to work. It makes poor lumber and everyone in Judea knows it.

Varro knew it too.

That was the point. Every person who watched the woodcutters come up this slope in the morning would understand what Rome was doing — not solving a timber shortage but making a statement. That the olive groves of Judea belonged to Rome. That a tree a family had tended for a hundred years could be taken in a morning and turned into an instrument of execution before the day was out. That nothing in this land, not the oldest tree, not the holiest ground, not the places where people had prayed and buried their dead and made their sanctuaries, was beyond Rome's use.

He had ordered an olive tree anyway.

This olive tree.

The one where Miriam and Benjamin had died. Where Thomas bar-Judah had knelt before the soldiers found him. Where a carpenter from Nazareth had stood one morning and set his hands against my bark and said something that had been in my grain ever since.

Rome did not need my wood. Rome chose my wood.

That is what I understood in the last hour of that evening, standing on my hillside with the light failing and one of Miriam's stones displaced and the city visible over the slope in the direction it had always been visible. The woodcutters would come in the morning not because I was the best timber available but because this hillside was a sanctuary and Varro wanted it to stop being one. Because the people who had gathered here and prayed here and bled here should understand that nothing in this land was too holy for Rome to put to use.

The night came. I stood in it the way I had stood in a hundred and twenty years of nights, roots in limestone, the city across the valley doing what cities do in the darkness.

I held the knowledge that came with the morning.

## They Came in the Morning

I felt them before I heard them.

This is what it is to have roots in limestone for over a century — you read the ground the way you read weather. The vibration of a single footstep is distinct from the vibration of a group. A group moving with purpose is distinct from a group in transit. These were moving with purpose, and they were carrying weight — I felt the additional press of metal tools against the soil through the feet of the men who carried them.

They came up my slope in the early morning while the light was still low and the road had not yet filled with the day's traffic. Three of them, then more behind. I counted by the footfalls.

I had been waiting for the next thing since the morning he set both hands against my bark and I understood that the waiting was nearly over. I had watched the road for weeks in the close attention that comes when you know something is coming but not when. The Passover traffic had swelled and then diminished. The city across the valley had moved through its particular intensity of that season and was settling back. And now these footsteps, purposeful, coming up my slope with the weight of iron.

I knew what they were. Not in theological terms. In the terms of a conscious being that has watched this hillside for over a hundred and twenty years and has observed, many times, men arriving with axes.

But I had never been the tree they came for.

## The Carpenter

The man who touched my bark first was their leader — I understood this from the way the others waited when he stepped forward. He was not young. His hands, when they reached my trunk, had the feel of hands that have worked wood for decades — the specific calluses, the economy of movement, the way they found the grain immediately without having to search for it.

He pressed his palms flat against my bark and held them there. Not the way most people touch a tree, which is briefly and without real attention. He held his hands there and was still, and I felt, through the contact, something I had not expected to feel from one of these men.

Conflict.

Not hesitation in the ordinary sense — not a man who is uncertain about his task. He knew his task. He was skilled at it and had done it many times. The conflict was something else: the particular grief of a person who is good at something and is being required to use that skill for a purpose they wish they did not have to serve.

I held this without judgment. He was here to cut me down. And yet what I felt through those hands was not threat but sorrow. His sorrow. And underneath it, so far underneath it that I might have imagined it, something else — a distant echo of what I had only felt once before. Not from him. Nothing in him was like what had entered my wood from that other contact. But adjacent to it somehow, as if the purpose he served, without knowing it, ran near the same ground.

I do not know if this is true. I am reporting what I felt.

He stepped back and said something to the others and they moved into position around me.

## The Cutting

The first axe struck.

I want to be honest about this: it was pain. I have felt many things in my long years on this hillside, and I have no category for the first axe strike except pain. The shock of it traveled from the point of impact through every ring of my heartwood. My roots felt it. My canopy felt it. Every bird that had been sitting in my branches was gone before the sound of the strike had fully moved through the air.

The second strike. The third.

What I found — and this I could not have predicted, I am reporting what happened — was that underneath the pain was something that was not pain. Not its absence. Not the absence of sensation. Something present alongside the pain that was different from it. The nearest I can come to it: the feeling, which I had known since the morning the first touch changed my wood, that I was aimed at something. That the consciousness that had been given to me without my asking, that had been mine since before I understood what it was, was aimed at something. That the thing it was aimed at was close.

The axes worked in rhythm. They were skilled. The carpenter directed them, moving around my trunk, assessing the cuts, redirecting when necessary. His hands that had carried grief when they touched my bark now carried competence. He was doing the thing he was good at. I was the material he worked.

My roots lost their grip on the limestone in stages. The wood at the base grew thinner. The canopy — the branch that had held its angle for so many decades, the nest sites, the accumulated seasons of leaf and shadow — swayed as my base weakened.

I had held a girl above dogs in the dark. I had held the martyrs' blood into my heartwood and felt it change me. I had held the contact of his hands through years of not understanding what it meant. I had held the knowledge that the waiting was almost over.

This I would hold too. Whatever came next, I would hold it the way I had held everything: with the full force of whatever I was, which was conscious, which was singular, which had been given to me for reasons I did not know and was not required to know.

The tree went over.

The hillside, which had been my entire world for over a hundred and twenty years, tilted and then was gone from me as my canopy struck the ground. The roots that had known limestone let go.

I was no longer rooted. I was wood. I was carried.

The thing I had been aimed at was ahead of me now, not behind. I could not see it. I could not name it. But I had been conscious long enough to know that what you cannot yet name is not therefore absent. It was there. It was close. And what I was — the consciousness, the capacity to hold, the singular fact of my awareness — was being carried toward it.


## The Shaping

The transport took two days. I arrived in a yard near the city's edge where timber was stored in ordered rows, and I was added to the rows and left to continue the drying that had been going on in my grain for years already.

Months passed.

I experienced them differently without roots. Living in the ground, I had perceived time through the slow chemistry of growth — the annual cycle of drawing and storing and releasing. Cut, I was still. The drying continued: moisture moving outward through my bark, the wood contracting slightly as it left, the grain settling into itself the way stone settles under its own weight. I grew denser and harder through those months of stillness.

Spring came again. Then spring of the thirty-third year.

Men came to the yard with specifications. They moved along the rows of timber assessing each piece and I felt their hands on me the same way I had felt the first carpenter's hands — purposeful, measuring. They chose me.

The construction was methodical.

The master carpenter marked the wood with chalk lines first, then saws following the marks, then chisels at the joint where the crossbeam would meet the upright. He worked with the precision of someone who had done this many times, who knew the measurements that satisfied the purpose. His apprentices shaped the smaller surfaces — the smoothing, the final fitting of the joint.

Each cut released more of the oil that had been built into my grain. The carpenter's apprentices commented on the smell. The master told them to keep working.

When they fitted the crossbeam to the upright and drove the joint tight, I felt the two pieces of what had been one tree become a single structure again — different in form from what I had been, but unified in the grain. The crossbeam had come from my largest lateral branch. The upright was my trunk. We had grown together for a hundred and twenty years. The joint held us together now by different means, but we were still the same wood.

The cross was finished before Passover.

It was moved to a storage area with other crosses and waited there in the spring air while the city filled with pilgrims. I was still and horizontal and drying and I waited.

I had been built for something. I did not know what. I knew only that whatever it was, the wood was ready.

It had been getting ready for a hundred and twenty years.
