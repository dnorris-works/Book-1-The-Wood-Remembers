# Chapter 17: The Dark Hours

It did not come the way night comes.

Night arrives from the east, the shadow of the world rolling in slow and predictable, the stars uncovering one by one as if they had been waiting. This came from everywhere at once. The shadows on my bark did not lengthen — they dissolved. The light did not fail gradually. It withdrew, the way a held breath withdraws before the cry.

The crowd fell quiet before the sky finished changing. I felt it through the ground first — the shuffling of feet going still, the small vibrations of bodies in motion ceasing one by one until the hillside was as quiet as it ever was before dawn in the deepest part of winter. People who had been shouting a moment before stood with their mouths closed. People who had been crouching over their game by the base of my trunk rose and looked up.

No one left. That was the thing I noticed. Every instinct should have driven them down the hill and back toward the gate. They stayed.

The temperature dropped. Not slowly, the way it drops in the late afternoon when the sun angles toward the western hills — but quickly, the warmth going out of the stone beneath me as if the stone had decided to keep it for itself. The women at my base drew their shawls tighter without looking away from him.

He was still breathing. I counted the intervals in the dark the way I had counted them in the light — by the shift of weight on my crossbeam, the small mechanical effort of push and rise. Slower now than before the dark came. But present.

I held him in my wood and I held the dark around him.

The silence had texture.

I had been on this hillside for over a century and I knew its silences. The silence before storms, which carries a particular pressure. The silence of deep winter nights, which has no weight at all and seems to stretch outward without limit. The silence of Sabbath mornings when the city below holds itself in deliberate stillness.

This was none of those. This silence had mass. It settled on the hill the way the dark had settled — not growing from the edges in, but present all at once, complete. The kind of silence that makes the small sounds louder: the shift of a soldier's armor, the breathing of the women below me, the flies still moving on the blood in my grain.

He made no sound for a long time.

I did not know how long. In the dark with no sun to measure by, time moved differently — not faster or slower exactly, but without the markers I was used to. The light usually told me where I was in the day. Without it I was only in the day, somewhere in the middle of it, holding what I was holding.

The soldiers had stopped moving entirely. Some had sat down. One leaned against the base of the hill with his head tipped back, looking at the sky the way people look when they are trying to understand something that has no explanation that they can reach.

Nothing explained this.

He had been silent for so long that when his voice came I felt it in my wood before I heard it.

A gathering of breath — longer, harder than any since the dark arrived. The push against the nail in his feet. The full effort of his chest. And then sound, not words yet, a sound that came from deep enough in him that it seemed to come from somewhere below the voice, from the place in a person where language has not yet separated itself from the thing it is trying to say.

Then words. Two words, repeated. His own language, not the Roman tongue, not the Greek the merchants used — the old language, the one the hillside had heard from shepherds for a thousand years.

The crowd heard it too. I felt the change move through them — the heads turning, the leaning forward. Someone near the base called out something, asking. Another voice answered. There was a brief confused movement, a few people stepping closer and then stopping.

The sound of his voice had been — I had no word for it then, and I had no word for it in all the centuries after. It was the sound of something at its absolute limit and past the limit and continuing past it. Not broken. Not collapsed. Present, past the edge of what presence can sustain.

The wind came then, for the first time since the darkness arrived. Only a movement, barely enough to stir the shawl of the woman nearest my base, but I felt it in my leaves and it carried the smell of rain from far off, from somewhere beyond the hills.

He said nothing more after that for a long time.

The light came back the way it had gone — all at once, not gradually.

One moment the hill was dark and the next the shadows were where they were supposed to be, long now, angled from the west, which meant the afternoon had gone further than I had realized. The air was still cold. The stone had not yet accepted the returning warmth.

The crowd moved again. Not leaving — shifting, the way people shift after a long stillness. Someone coughed. The soldiers' voices came back, low and practical, the sounds of men returning to a task they had put down.

His breathing had changed again while I could not see the sky to measure it. It was shallower now. The intervals between the shifts of weight on my crossbeam were longer than they had been, and when the breath came it came with a sound I had not heard in the hours before the dark. A catching sound. A sound that cost something at the base of the throat.

The women at my base had not moved through any of it. They stood in the same arrangement they had held since morning — the nearest with her hand pressed flat against my bark, which I had not registered until the light returned and I felt the warmth of her palm against the wood.

She had been holding on to me.

I did not know if it helped her. I was only wood. But I held still and let her hold.
