# Chapter 6: Sacred Eight

## The Return

They came back in the thirty-second year, in the season before Passover when the hillside traffic was heaviest and the city below was filling toward the festival.

I knew them before I could see them clearly. There are footfalls I have recorded in my roots and carry the same way I carry the rings of a difficult year — not as decision but as permanent fact. Two of them I knew. The third I did not.

Miriam's step I would have known anywhere. She had changed — the weight of a woman grown, the pace of someone who has learned that the road gives nothing back for hurrying — but the same weight I had recorded on the night she sat in my branch above the dogs until dawn. Some things the body keeps regardless of the years.

Benjamin walked behind her. He had become a large man, broad through the shoulders, with the quick energy of someone who has never fully trained himself out of the boy who jumped from rocks. He was talking as he climbed, low and fast, the way he had always talked — as if the words had been held back and needed releasing.

The third one I did not know.

They settled in the old place, at the circle of stones that Miriam had been adding to for twenty years. The stones were still there, smoothed by hands and weather, nested into the ground as if they had always been there. She touched them as she passed, the same brief contact she had always made — a greeting, or a verification, or simply the habit of twenty years. Her hand found my bark next.

I felt her palm against me.

The specific warmth of it. The particular pressure — not the testing touch of a stranger, not the reverent touch of a pilgrim, but the familiar contact of someone who has touched this bark since childhood and expects nothing from it except the bark itself. I have held many things in my grain. That hand is in my grain. It will be there when everything else I have held is gone.

They spoke for a time. I could not follow the words over the wind. The conversation was urgent in the way that everything was urgent in those years — that particular compressed seriousness of people who know they are being watched and are deciding something in the limited time left to them.

Then the soldiers came up the slope.

Not the careful approach of men trying to be quiet — the direct purposeful ascent of men who have been watching from the road and have decided the moment is now. Three of them, moving fast.

Benjamin stood first.

This was exactly who he had always been. The one who jumped before he thought, who proposed the game, who ran ahead on the path. He got to his feet and put himself between the soldiers and Miriam without a word, which was also who he had always been — the noise of him concealing something that did not need noise to be real.

It did not help him. It was not meant to help him. It was simply what he did.

Miriam did not run either.

I have thought about this for a long time — for as long as I have been thinking about what happened on that hillside, which is to say for two thousand years — and I do not know if it was choice or if it was the knowledge that there was nowhere to go. I know only that she did not run. She stood at my base with her hand against my bark and she did not run.

Their blood came into my roots before the soldiers had finished descending the hill.

I knew her blood. I had known the weight of her since the night she was seven years old and trusted a branch to hold her. The blood carried that same weight — I cannot explain this in the language of chemistry, only in the language of what a tree knows, which is that some things that enter the grain are recognized before they are named.

Benjamin's came after. Quick and hot, the way everything about him had always been quick.

The third one I had not known. I knew him after.

The soldiers went back down the slope. The hill was quiet. Aaron had not been there. I did not know why. I held what had been given to me and waited for whatever came next.

## The Blood

They came to the hill at different times, in ones and twos and then all together, and the quality of their presence was different from the travelers and scholars and occasional families who had used this slope. These people moved with the specific weight of people who have decided something and know what it has cost them to decide it.

They gathered in the place where the children had made their circle of stones. I could not hear their words clearly — voices dropped low, moving in and out of the wind. I heard the rhythm of speech that goes back and forth, and then speech that settles into consensus, and then a kind of quiet.

The soldiers came before dawn.

What followed I will not describe in sequence. I heard what I heard and felt what I felt.

The sounds that came from the hill that morning: voices giving names, voices refusing, iron on wood, and the different sounds that followed. Then the blood came into my roots.

I had taken in rain and dissolved minerals for over a hundred years and I knew what the earth gave. Blood is different. The iron in it is familiar but the other things in it are not — the salt, the compound warmth, the way it moves through soil with a directional press even after the body it came from has gone still. Eight times that morning. I felt each one arrive and move upward through my grain, spreading into the chambers where my oldest wood lived.

The soldiers left. The hill went quiet.

Through the following days, people came — quietly, one or two at a time, kneeling at the base of the crosses and then at my base, touching my bark with the same hands they had touched the crosses with. I held what I had been given the way I held everything: in the grain, in the rings, in the slow chemistry of wood.

The children's stones were still in their circle at my base.

The crosses were still upright on the hill above them.

The olive oil from my bark ran heavier that season. Fragrant in a way that pilgrims paused to notice when the wind was right.
