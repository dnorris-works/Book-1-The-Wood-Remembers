# Chapter 22: The Lance

## Verification

The soldiers had work left to do.

I had known they would. I had watched enough deaths on this hill to understand the protocol — the body had to be confirmed before Sabbath, before the families could claim what remained, before the hill could be cleared of its evidence. Two men still lived on either side of him, their breathing audible in the quiet that had settled after the last word. I could hear the discussion at the base of the hill, the specific tone of men reviewing a task list, before I heard the footsteps.

They went to the men on either side first.

I will not describe what they did. I had felt it before on this hill, the sudden shock traveling up through the wood into the posts when bone gives — a sound and a sensation that have no good name and that I did not want to hold in my memory alongside everything else from this day. The men on either side of him cried out. Then they did not.

The soldiers moved toward the center post.

One of them looked up at what I was holding. He stopped. Said something to the man beside him, too low for me to parse the words, and the other one looked up too. A longer pause. Then the one who had spoken walked away and came back with someone else — a different posture, a different weight to the footsteps, the kind of deliberateness that comes with rank.

The senior man studied what I was holding. He circled the base of my post slowly, looking. He was not in a hurry. Whatever he was assessing, he was being careful about it.

He called for the lance.

## The Wound

I had no warning before the impact. Not the way I had felt the nails — those had come with the sound of the mallet first, a sequence I'd had a fraction of a moment to brace against. The lance was just the thrust, sudden and complete, entering the side of what I held and driving through at an upward angle.

The shock traveled through the wood.

Not like the nails. The nails had been compression, the grain splitting and accepting and holding. This was lateral — a vibration moving out from the point of entry through my crossbeam and into the upright, a single hard pulse that settled almost immediately into stillness.

Then the liquids came.

I felt them before I saw them — warmth moving down the wood, two different textures in the same stream. The blood I already knew from the long hours of the day. This was different, mixed with something thinner, something that ran ahead of the blood and then blended with it as both found the grain and followed it down.

I did not understand it. I have thought about it for two thousand years and I still cannot explain it in the language of living things. The body had been dead for some time. The blood should have been settling, pooling, not flowing. What came from that wound flowed.

Down my wood. Into the cracks the nails had made. Into the grain.

The women at the base of my post were still there. The young man with them. They watched the flow and none of them spoke.

The senior soldier took a step back. I felt the shift in his weight, the way a person steps back when something they have seen does not match their category for it. He was a man who had done this work for years, I would guess. He had confirmed many deaths on many hills.

He said something.

The soldiers near him went quiet. The ones who had been moving to collect tools stopped moving. The words passed through the group the way a cold comes through a room when a door opens — something changed, and everyone felt it, and no one immediately knew what to do with it.

The women at my base did not look away.

## After

The sun was close to the horizon by the time the men came for the body.

Two of them — well dressed, moving with the purposefulness of people who have arranged something and are now executing the arrangement. They had spoken with someone in authority first; I had heard the exchange at a distance, the back-and-forth of permission being given. One of them carried linen. The other had brought spices, the smell of myrrh arriving before he did.

The nails came out differently than they had gone in. Going in, each one had been driven with the full force of intention. Coming out, someone had to work at them — careful, effortful, the grain releasing what it had been made to hold. I felt each one leave as a specific loss of pressure, a small collapse of what had been held so tightly for so long.

The body was lowered. The women received it.

I stood empty.

I had held living things before — birds, the press of hands, the weight of generations climbing into my branches. I had held the dead too, over the years on this hill. But I had never held something and then given it back and felt the giving-back like this — not relief, not release, but a specific absence, the shape of what was no longer there still present in the grain.

The nail holes remained.

The women worked quickly in the failing light, wrapping, preparing, doing what they had come to do. The men helped carry the body down the far side of the hill toward the garden where the tomb was. The soldiers had gone. The crowd that had spent the day on the hillside was entirely gone now, thinned away during the long quiet hours after the last word.

The hill was empty except for the other two crosses, which held what they held.

And me.

The blood had dried in my grain — both the blood from the long hours of the day and what had come with the water from the lance wound. The grain had accepted it the way wood accepts rain, drawing it in, making it part of itself. I could feel the difference between the wood I had been this morning and the wood I was now. Not damage. Something else. A change in the grain that went deeper than the surface cracks the nails had made.

The light failed.

Somewhere in the city, the lamps were being lit for Sabbath. The smell of the first fires reached me — bread, oil, the ordinary warmth of households beginning the ordinary evening. The hill cooled as the sky darkened. Stars appeared, the familiar ones I had watched across decades, in the same positions they had always been in.

I stood in the dark and held what the day had left in me.

I did not know, then, what it was. I knew it was not nothing.
