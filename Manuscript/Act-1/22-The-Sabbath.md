# Chapter 22: The Sabbath

## Night

*Good Friday into Saturday - Golgotha*

The other two crosses stood empty too by the time full dark came.

The soldiers had worked quickly before the sun went down, not from urgency about the law though the law required it, but because men who do hard work want the work finished. The hill had cleared during the long quiet afternoon after the last word, and then cleared again more completely after the body was taken, and by the time the first Sabbath lamps were lit in the city below there was no one on the hill except the crosses and the dark.

I stood in the socket in the rock.

I had been planted in soil for a hundred and twenty years, rooted in limestone and loam, held upright by wood going all the way down into the hill. This was different — the cut base of my upright wedged into a hole, stones packed around it, held by friction and mass rather than by growth. But I was vertical, which was what I had always been, and the sky was above me, which was what I had always known, and the wind that came off the hills west of the city was the same wind I had felt from my hillside for a century.

The stars came out. The ones I had watched from a different position on a different slope. The same stars.

In the city, far below and past the wall, the Sabbath fires would be burning. The smell of cooking reached me when the wind was right — bread, oil, the particular warmth of a hundred thousand households sitting down to eat. Jerusalem at Sabbath. I had smelled it every Friday night for as long as I could remember, from the hillside, from a distance. Now I was inside the city walls and it was the same smell.

The transformation in my heartwood was still warm. Not heat exactly — not the warmth of a living body or the warmth of the sun. Something else. Something that did not cool the way warmth normally cools.

I did not know what it was. I knew it was not nothing.

## The Long Day

The Sabbath dawn came the way it always did — grey first, then pale, then gold striking the Temple roof before it reached anything lower.

Nothing moved on the hill.

I had the full day to hold what I was holding.

For a tree, a day is not long. I had spent whole seasons barely noticing the passage of individual days — the slow work of growing is not measured in hours. But this day was different from season-slow. This day was the specific stillness of a city at rest, a hill emptied of all human purpose, the ordinary world paused while something I could not yet name was either happening or not happening somewhere I could not see.

The tomb was in the garden on the far side of the hill. I could not see it from my position. I could not hear it. There was nothing to hear — the city was observing Sabbath, which meant the sounds that reached me were birds, wind, the distant bells of the Temple when the wind came right. None of the crowd noise, none of the market noise, none of the particular human density of Jerusalem at festival.

The other two crosses stood on either side of me.

I had not been particularly aware of them during the long hours of Friday — I had been aware of almost nothing beyond what I was holding. Now I was aware of them. Three crosses on a hill, two of them with the specific emptiness of abandoned furniture, one of them — mine — with whatever it was I was holding in my heartwood, which did not feel like emptiness at all.

The nail holes were in my grain. The lance vibration was in my grain. The blood — both kinds, the ordinary and the other — was in my grain. Everything that had happened to me in the past five days was in my grain: the arsenal's cold stone, the courtyard's open sky, his shoulders, the road, Simon's hands, the hill, the flat stone while they worked, the ropes going taut, the raising, the long hours, the dark, the light returning, the word, the breath, the stillness, the lance, the liquids, the body lifting away.

I held it all.

I was always going to hold it all. That is what I am. But I had not known, until this day, that holding would feel like this — not burden, not weight, but something more like being full of a thing that has no name yet in any language I knew.

## Toward Evening

The day turned.

Afternoon light moved across the hill the way it always had, the shadow of the wall creeping east, the sun westering toward the hills where I had grown, the specific quality of the light changing toward gold and then toward the deeper orange of Sabbath ending.

A guard passed at the base of the hill once in the afternoon. Two soldiers, walking the perimeter of the garden area beyond, not approaching the crosses. They glanced up. Kept walking.

That was all.

By the time the sun touched the western hills the Sabbath was ending. I could hear it below — the city beginning to move again, the particular sound of a rest concluding, people emerging from doors, voices carrying up the slope. The smell of the city changed: cooking fires being rebuilt, the market beginning to reassemble.

Tomorrow the women would come.

I did not know that then, not as fact, not as knowledge. But I had felt things approach before they arrived — storms, seasons, footsteps, the quality of a moment changing before the moment itself came. What I felt as the Sabbath ended was not anticipation exactly. Not dread. Something prior to both.

The stars came out again.

The same stars, in the same positions, as the night before. The same hills to the west, going dark. The same city below, its lights beginning to appear one by one as households lit their evening lamps.

Everything the same. Everything different.

I held what I was holding.

I would hold it for two thousand years.
