# Chapter 12: Sacred Waiting

## The Arsenal

*Holy Week 33 CE*

I had never been inside before.

One hundred and twenty years on the hillside northwest of the city — open sky, limestone underfoot, wind coming off the hills at dusk — and now stone walls on four sides and the floor cold against my grain and the ceiling so low the room seemed to press down. The chamber smelled of iron and old wood and something sharper underneath, oil or pitch. Light came through two narrow windows set high in the wall, rectangular strips that moved across the floor as the day turned and vanished entirely at night.

The fortress was loud in ways the hillside had never been. Boots on stone at all hours — the watch changes, the patrols. Metal sounds I had no name for. Voices in Latin carrying the particular flat tone of men reporting to other men. Underneath all of it, constant and low, the sound of the city: thousands of people moving through the Passover preparations below the walls, more voices, more feet, donkeys, the specific carrying cry of merchants in the markets.

I lay horizontal among other timber. Cedar from somewhere far north — I could smell the difference, a colder resin, nothing like the oil in my own grain. Oak planks. Long iron bars stacked against the far wall. I had spent my whole life vertical, reaching upward, and now the ceiling was above me and the floor held my weight and there was nothing to reach toward.

I was not afraid. I am not sure fear is what I mean. But I noticed the difference.

## The Days

Four days passed in that chamber. I counted them by the light in the narrow windows — the pale arrival of morning, the slow crawl of the strips across the floor, the grey thinning before the dark.

The city below the fortress grew louder each day as the pilgrims arrived for the festival. By the third day the noise was constant, even at night — fires burning in the streets, singing at a distance, the particular rising sound of large crowds compressing into narrow spaces. Jerusalem took in tens of thousands for Passover. I could feel their presence through the stone floor the way I had once felt roots moving through the hillside, a general pressure distributed across everything.

A soldier came through the storage chamber each morning to check the materials. He spent more time near my section than the rest. Not performing any particular task — looking, mostly. The second morning he ran his hand along my grain without looking around to see if anyone was watching. His hand was rough-palmed and warm. He didn't say anything. After a moment he moved on.

I thought about the hillside. I thought about the specific light in the hour before dawn when Jerusalem's walls caught the first grey rising and the dew on my leaves made the air taste clean. I thought about the three children and the night I held Miriam while the dogs circled below, the way the stars looked from that position, the cold coming up from the ground and the weight of her pressed into my branches.

That was a different kind of weight than what was coming. I understood that, though I did not know the shape of what was coming. I could feel it the way I had always felt a particular storm before it arrived — not the content, just the size of it, the quality of the pressure building in the air.

## The Night

On the last night, something changed in the city.

It began late — past the deepest hour, when the festival fires had burned low and most of the sound had settled. A disturbance, distant at first, voices moving fast through streets, the kind of purposeful movement that has a destination. Then torches visible through the high windows, not the steady glow of street fires but moving light, organized, heading somewhere.

The soldier who slept in the corridor outside the storage room woke. I heard him get up, heard him speak to someone, heard a reply he didn't seem to expect. More footsteps after that, several of them, going in the same direction.

The city quieted eventually. Not the peaceful quiet of the night settling — a different quiet. The quiet of something completed.

I lay in the dark on the cold stone and waited for the light to come back through the narrow windows. It would. The windows had showed me four mornings already, reliable as they had been.

The fifth morning would be different. I could feel that too, the same way I had always known weather.
