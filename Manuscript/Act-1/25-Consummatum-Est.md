# Chapter 25: Consummatum Est

## The Heartwood

The soldiers had stopped looking up.

There was nothing to watch anymore. The body on my crossbeam was still in the way a body is still when stillness is permanent — no micro-shifts, no evidence of breath distributed through the wood, no heartbeat reaching me through the grain. I had learned the difference over the long hours of the afternoon. This was the other kind of stillness.

The women below had not moved.

The hill was quieter than it had been at any point in the day. Not the forced quiet of the darkness, which had pressed down with weight — this was ordinary late-afternoon quiet, the kind that follows when the event is over and the crowd has not yet decided to leave. A few people were talking at the hill's edge in low voices. The soldiers murmured among themselves. The bird that had been audible earlier was not audible now.

His blood in my wood had nearly stopped moving. The lines it had traced along my grain through the long hours — following the rings outward from the nail holes, traveling the channels the wood offered — had slowed to the last seep of a thing almost finished.

Then it didn't finish.

The warmth remained.

I have held many things through my years on this hillside. I know what warmth does when the thing producing it has ended — the way the stone cools after the sun moves, the way a body loses heat after it has stopped. What was in my grain did not behave the way warmth behaves. It did not dissipate outward. It did not follow the ordinary path of warmth, which is always away from itself, always thinning, always returning toward the temperature of the surrounding air.

It stayed. And then it moved — not outward but inward, not toward the surface but toward the center, toward the oldest wood, the wood I grew in the earliest years on the hillside when the roots were still learning the limestone.

I do not know what this was. I had no name for it then. Whatever name belongs to it, I have had two thousand years to find the name and I am still not certain I have it exactly right. What I can tell you is what I felt: something that behaved unlike any warmth I had known, moving in a direction warmth does not move, settling in the part of me that was oldest and densest and most myself.

And then being still there. Present. Like a stone placed at the bottom of a well — not going anywhere. Not thinning. Not following the ordinary rules of things that are placed and then disperse.

I stood with the body on my crossbeam and the ordinary afternoon continuing around us — the soldiers, the women, the city visible over the walls, the sun continuing its path toward the western hills — and I held what was in my grain, which was something I did not understand and which did not ask to be understood.


## What Passed Through

Then something else happened.

I do not know how to place it in time exactly — whether it was before the warmth stopped moving or after, whether it was the same moment or a different one. Time on that hill, in those hours, did not behave the way time behaves on an ordinary afternoon. What I know is that it happened, and that it was not the warmth, and that it was not anything I had felt before or have felt since.

Something passed through.

Not through the wood the way the nails had passed through, which was violence and compression and the grain splitting to accommodate iron. Not through the wood the way his blood had passed through, which was warmth and chemistry and the slow absorption of one thing into another. This was different from both of those in a way I cannot account for, because what passed through was not a physical thing passing through a physical substance. It was contact of a kind that the wood had no mechanism to receive, and yet the wood received it.

I have spent two thousand years trying to find the word for it. There is no word. There are words that approach it from different directions — presence, opening, the end of a distance that had not previously been known to exist — but none of them hold what actually happened. They describe the shape of the absence the thing left rather than the thing itself.

What I can tell you is what my grain registered in the moment of contact: everything it had ever held — the limestone of the hillside, the rain of a hundred winters, Miriam's hands, the martyrs' blood, the warmth that had just settled into my heartwood — all of it was still present, still itself, and simultaneously this other thing was also present, which did not displace any of it but existed alongside it in a way that should not have been possible. Two things occupying the same space. Both completely real.

It lasted for a moment that I cannot measure.

Then it was simply in the grain. The way the blood was in the grain. The way the warmth was in the grain. Present. Not dispersing. Not asking to be understood.

I held it the way I held everything: completely, without knowing what I was holding.

The women below had not moved. The soldiers were still there. The sun continued toward the western hills. Nothing on the hill indicated that anything had happened. And yet everything in my grain knew that it had.

It was enough to hold it.

That is all I knew then. That I was holding something, and the holding was mine, and whatever the something was, it was not going to leave.

The vigil was over.
