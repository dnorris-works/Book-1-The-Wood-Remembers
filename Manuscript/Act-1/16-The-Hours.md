# Chapter 16: The Hours

## The Weight of Breathing

The weight was not what I expected. I had held heavy things — stone, rain-soaked earth pressing down on my roots after floods, the cumulative dampness of decades. But this was different. His weight came and went with each breath.

To exhale he had to push against the nail in his feet and lift his chest. I felt it as a shift of pressure along my vertical beam — a small rise, then the slow release as he could not sustain it. Up and down. Up and down. Each breath a decision made in wood and nail and bone.

I counted them without meaning to.

The women below my base had not moved. The nearest stood close enough that her shawl grazed my bark when the wind shifted, and her breathing was steadier than his, though not by much. She did not speak. She was not going to speak. She was the kind of person who stays.

The mockers had grown quieter as the morning wore on — not kinder, only tired of noise that produced no response. A few soldiers crouched near the base of the hill playing a game with marked pieces. Stone on stone. A pause. Stone on stone again.

His blood had followed the grain of my crossbeam in slow lines. Wood drinks what it is given. I had drunk rain for a hundred years, and limestone water, and dew that carried the smell of the valley in the hours before dawn. This was different. It went deep before it dried.

I held him. The sun moved. The shadows shortened.

## What I Heard

He spoke three times in the morning hours. I felt each word before I heard it — a gathering in his chest against the weight, a push against the nail, the muscles of his throat working, and then sound, hoarse with effort, carrying further than it should have.

The first time I could not make out the words. Only the sound of them moving outward across the crowd. Some of the mockers stopped. A few people looked at one another. Then the sound settled and the day went on.

The second time he spoke toward the man dying on the other side of me — there were two, one on each flank, but only one had said anything to him, and only once, and his voice had been quiet the way a man's voice gets when he has stopped hoping for anything except one small thing. I felt the effort it cost him to answer. The breath bought with the nail. The words that followed.

I held them the way wood holds sound in its grain — absorbs and does not repeat.

The third time was for the woman below. He spoke her name and another name, and his voice was lower than before, more air than sound, and when it was finished I felt the weight shift on my crossbeam and settle again. The woman below made a sound that was not a cry. It was something quieter than a cry and harder to bear.

The flies had found the blood on my wood. Their sound was constant, without meaning, the way the sound of water is constant without meaning. The sun pressed everything flat and hot.

## The Slow Hours

By the time the sun had passed its highest point and begun its fall toward the western hills, his breathing had changed.

In the first hours it had been labored but regular — I could feel the rhythm of it in the load on my crossbeam, the small mechanical push and rise that cost him what it cost him. By the middle of the afternoon the rhythm had broken. The pauses between rises grew longer. Sometimes I counted to eight, to ten, before the weight shifted and the breath came.

The women below had not moved. A young man had joined them — not old enough for the soldiers to pay much attention. He stood just behind the women and did not take his eyes from the crossbeam above him.

The heat had built through the hours until the limestone of the hill gave it back from below as hard as it came from above. The smell of the place — old stone, old blood, the residue of everything this hill had held across the years — had risen with the heat until it was its own thing, heavy and particular.

I felt his weight in my wood. I felt the small shudders that moved through him between breaths. I felt the grain of my crossbeam where his wrists pressed against the iron. These things I could feel as a tree feels — not with understanding, but with the absolute attention of matter to matter, as root feels water, as bark feels cold.

Something was gathering. I had no word for it.

## Before Dark

The light changed before the darkness came.

I had seen a thousand sunsets from this hillside, the slow warmth going out of the western sky, the blue deepening toward black in the east while Jerusalem's walls caught the last of the gold. This was not that. The light did not redden. It did not gather in the west.

It simply went quiet.

The shadows on my bark blurred as if the source of them had grown uncertain. The women below looked up — not at him, at the sky. The soldiers' game stopped. No one spoke.

His breathing was very slow now. Each rise bought at greater cost than the last.

I had no word for what was happening in the light above us. I had stood through storms that turned midday dark, through winter mornings when cloud came off the hills and swallowed everything, through those deepest hours before dawn. This was none of those things.

It pressed down. It had weight. The way his weight had weight — not passive, but present.

I held him in my wood. The darkness came.
