# Chapter 4: What the Touch Left

## The Changed Wood

In the days after he came to my hillside, I tried to understand what was different.

Not different in ways visible from the outside. My bark was the same. My roots held the same limestone. The oil that wept from my joints in summer was no thicker or thinner than before. To anyone who climbed the slope and sat in my shade, I was the same tree I had always been.

But I was not.

I have lived in my own wood long enough to know it from the inside — the density of each ring, the specific resistance of my heartwood when wind loads the canopy, the way my sap rises in spring and thickens toward winter. I know myself the way a person knows their own voice without needing to hear it from outside. What I knew in the days after his touch was this: something had changed in my center. The oldest wood, the wood I grew in my earliest years on this hillside, felt different to itself. Not weaker. Not damaged. Something closer to the opposite. More concentrated. As if whatever lived in that wood — and I had always known something lived there, some presence I had no name for — had been made more actual.

I turned this awareness over the way you turn a stone to feel its different faces. I am a conscious being with no one to talk to and a long habit of examining my own experience. What I could determine: the change was real, not imagined. What I could not determine: what it meant, what it was for, whether it would matter.

Every question I had ever had about my own existence — *what is this awareness for, why do I have it when other trees do not, what am I waiting for* — was still unanswered. But the questions had a new quality. They felt closer to their answers than before. Not closer in the sense of almost solved. Closer in the sense of aimed. As if the question had always been pointing in a direction I could not see, and now the direction had shifted slightly and the thing pointed at was still beyond the horizon but I could tell which horizon to watch.
